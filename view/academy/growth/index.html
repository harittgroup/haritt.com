Growth vs brand marketing

Binet & Field, The Long and the Short of It (2013); Sharp, How Brands Grow (2010)

What it meansA neighbourhood bakery has a trusted signboard and a "10% off today" chalk sign. Brand marketing is the signboard: it makes people remember and trust you. Performance marketing is the chalk sign: it buys one clear action, like a click or a sale, at a cost you can see. Growth marketing treats the whole customer path, from first contact to repeat purchase and referral, as something to improve through experiments. The IPA (a UK advertising body) found the two kinds work best together.

Why it mattersRun only chalk-sign campaigns and you hit a ceiling. You're harvesting demand that already exists, and costs creep up. Run only signboard work and you can't prove what helped. Knowing which job each activity does tells you how to judge it, and how long to wait.

How to use itSort each activity into demand creation (brand, content, community, PR, meaning press and public relations) or demand capture (search ads, retargeting, emails to people who already asked). Judge capture on cost per result. Judge creation on reach, branded search and direct visits over months. On a small budget, start with capture and your own channels, because they pay back sooner and are measurable from day one. See for how both fit the customer path.

The marketing funnel and the customer journey

Lewis (1898), AIDA; Kotler, Marketing 4.0 (2016)

What it meansThink of a tuition class. Many parents hear about it, fewer visit, fewer still enrol, and only some stay for the next year. That narrowing is the marketing funnel. The classic version is AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. Newer versions say awareness, consideration, conversion, then retention (they come back) and advocacy (they recommend you). Teams also say TOFU, MOFU and BOFU for top, middle and bottom. The customer journey is the messier real version, where people loop and compare.

Why it mattersEach stage needs its own message and its own number to watch. Ask for the sale while someone is only just hearing of you, and you waste money. Keep talking about your brand when they're ready to decide, and you lose the sale.

How to use itDraw a one-page journey map. Put the stages across the top. Under each, write what the customer does, thinks and asks, and where they meet you (search, social, referral, website, WhatsApp, a call). Mark the places people drop off. Give each stage one metric and one owner, with numbers from GA4 and your lead sheet. Fix the weakest stage first, not the biggest. See for a startup version.

Pirate metrics (AARRR)

McClure, Startup Metrics for Pirates (2007)

What it meansA small app owner hears "we need more traffic", but the real leak may be that people install and never open it. AARRR, nicknamed pirate metrics because it sounds like a pirate, checks five stages. Acquisition: people arrive. Activation: they have a good first experience. Retention: they come back. Referral: they tell others. Revenue: they pay. Some teams put Revenue before Referral. Each stage gets one conversion rate and one clear event that counts.

Why it mattersWithout this, "traffic is low" becomes the answer to everything, and you buy visitors who leave at the next step. Seeing the stages side by side shows where growth is stuck. Often the fix is activation, not more traffic.

How to use itDefine each stage as something you can track, then work out the rate from one stage to the next. For an app, activation might be "first plan created". For a service business, acquisition is an enquiry and activation is a qualified call. Put the five numbers on one sheet or in a GA4 funnel report (see ). Pick the lowest stage, run experiments on it (), and measure again. Real growth also comes from loops ().

North Star metric and input metrics

Ellis; Amplitude, North Star Playbook; Croll & Yoskovitz, Lean Analytics (2013)

What it meansA gym owner could watch revenue, footfall or Instagram likes. The one number that really shows members getting value might be "members who train three times a week". That's a North Star metric: the single number that best shows the value customers get and predicts long-term revenue. It isn't revenue itself. Input metrics are the few things you can move to push it up: breadth (how many), depth (how much each), frequency (how often) and efficiency (how fast or cheap).

Why it mattersWithout one shared number, everyone chases a different target and pulls in different directions. A good North Star points product, marketing and sales at the same result. It also protects you from vanity metrics, like total sign-ups or page views, which only look good.

How to use itTest a candidate with four questions. Does it show customers getting value? Does it come before revenue, not after? Can your team move it every week? Can anyone understand it? Then split it into inputs, each with an owner, and review them weekly (). For your current stage, pick one metric that matters (OMTM), and prefer actionable metrics that change what you do. Keep revenue and cost as guardrails.

Product-market fit and message-market fit

Andreessen, The Pmarca Guide to Startups (2007); Ellis, Sean Ellis test

What it meansSay you open a cloud kitchen. If customers order once and vanish, no ad budget saves it. If they reorder weekly, growth gets easy. Product-market fit (PMF) means that second case: a good market and a product it truly satisfies. A simple check, the Sean Ellis test, asks users how they'd feel if they could no longer use the product. If at least 40 percent say "very disappointed", you've probably reached fit (a rule of thumb). Message-market fit (MMF) comes first: does your message make one group respond?

Why it mattersScale paid ads before fit and you just pay for people who leave. Fit decides whether your growth work builds up or fades away. Without message-market fit, even a good product gets ignored.

How to use itLook for three signals together. Survey: ask the Ellis question to people who've used the product a few times. Behaviour: the retention curve flattens instead of falling to zero (). Pull: people arrive through referrals or search without being pushed. To test your message, run a small landing page or ad test with two or three promises and see which gets replies (). For services, fit looks like repeat clients, referrals and a steady win rate.

Signs of fit

Users come back without reminders. Survey answers cluster around one benefit. Prospects describe the problem in your words. Sales get quicker.

False positives

Sign-ups from a one-time promotion. Praise from friends and family. High traffic with no repeat use. One happy customer who never repeats.

Growth loops vs funnels

Balfour; Reforge, growth loops

What it meansA funnel is like a tank you must refill daily. A growth loop refills itself. Say a tuition class gets a result, the parent tells friends, and the friends join. Those new students create the next round. A funnel runs one way and nothing flows back. In a loop, what one round produces feeds the next. Common types: viral (invites), content (pages bring search visitors), paid (revenue buys ads), and sales or partner.

Why it mattersA funnel needs fresh spend all the time to stay full. A loop compounds, so the same effort gives you more over time. Thinking in loops also shows what to build, like shareable results, proof pages and referral features, not just what to buy.

How to use itWrite your loop as steps: input, action, output, reinvestment. Measure the conversion at each step and how long one round takes. Improve the weakest step first. A loop only works if what it returns is worth more than it costs, so check your unit economics (). Start with one loop, since most small teams can't run several. See for viral loops in detail.

Paid metrics and the small-budget test

Standard advertising metrics

What it meansThink of a pizza stall: people walk past, some stop, some buy. Paid ads work the same way, as a short chain of simple ratios. What a result costs equals what a click costs divided by the share of clicks that convert. Whether that cost is good depends only on your margin and what a customer is worth to you ().

Why it mattersWithout these numbers, spending is guessing. Each metric points at a different problem. A low CTR (share of people who click) means a weak ad or audience. A high CPC (cost per click) with a low CVR (share of clicks that convert) means a weak page or offer. A high CPM (cost per 1,000 views) means an expensive audience.

How to use itRun a small-budget test like a mini experiment. Write your guess and change one thing. Set the budget at the most you can afford to lose, plus success and stop rules, before you start. Don't edit mid-test, and wait for enough clicks to read: a handful of leads is noise (, ). Log the result () and get approval before spending. Example only, say: spend 2,000, get 100 clicks and 5 leads. Then CPC = 20, CVR = 5 percent, CPL = 400, and CPL = CPC / CVR holds.

Attribution

Kaushik; Google and Meta documentation

What it meansA customer sees your Instagram ad, later opens your email, then searches your name and buys. Who gets the credit? Attribution is the rule that decides. A touchpoint is any contact, like an ad, email or search visit, and the lookback window is how far back a touch can still earn credit. MTA (multi-touch attribution) shares credit across several touches. MMM (marketing mix modelling) uses statistics on total spend and sales, without tracking individual users.

Why it mattersThe model you pick changes which channels look good. Email and branded search often shine under last touch simply because they come last. Content and social often shine under first touch because they come first. Pick badly and you may cut the channel that started the sale.

How to use itNo model shows the truth, and each ad platform counts its own conversions, so totals overshoot. Word of mouth and private sharing (dark social) never show in click data. For real cause and effect, run an incrementality test: hold back a group (a holdout) and compare. Google's data-driven model needs enough conversions. Last click plus self-reported is a sound start.

A/B testing and statistics

Kohavi, Tang & Xu, Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020)

What it meansPicture a bakery handing out two flyers on alternate customers, then counting who comes back. That is an A/B test: you randomly split visitors into two groups. One sees the current version, the control (A). The other sees a changed version, a variant (B). You compare one metric. Because the split is random, the change caused the gap, and statistics tells you if the gap is bigger than plain chance.

Why it mattersComparing "before" with "after" fools you. Say sales rise after a new page, but it was also festival season. Only a random split running at the same time isolates your change. Note that Google Optimize shut down in September 2023, so use other testing tools (some paid, so Small budget) or a coded split with GA4.

How to use itFix your numbers before you start. A handy rule: visitors per variant n ≈ 16 × σ² ÷ Δ². Here σ² is how much the metric varies and Δ is the smallest change you care about. For a conversion rate p, σ² = p × (1 − p). Example: say baseline 5%, and you want to spot +1 point: n ≈ 16 × 0.0475 ÷ 0.0001 = 7,600 per variant. Run whole weeks, don't stop at the first win (peeking), and check the split matches your plan.

CAC, LTV and payback

Fader, Customer Centricity (2012); Skok, For Entrepreneurs

What it meansA bakery spends a month handing out flyers and free samples, and 10 new regulars walk in. What each one cost you is your CAC (customer acquisition cost): sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers won. LTV (also CLV, customer lifetime value) is the margin a customer leaves with you over their whole stay. Payback period is how long that margin takes to repay the CAC.

Why it mattersWithout these numbers you can grow and still go broke. If winning a customer costs more than they ever return, every sale digs the hole deeper. Even a profitable customer can lock up your cash for months before it comes back. And averages hide your best and worst customers.

How to use itAdd up every cost for fully loaded CAC: ads, tools, agency fees, content, and the time of whoever sells. Paid CAC counts only paid spend and paid customers; blended CAC counts everything. Work from margin, not revenue. For a subscription, LTV is monthly margin ÷ monthly churn (the share who leave each month). An LTV ÷ CAC near 3 is a rule of thumb, not a law. Early LTV is a guess, so track real customers (see ).

MetricFormula
CACAcquisition spend ÷ new customers
Gross margin per customer per monthMonthly revenue × gross margin %
LTV (subscription)Monthly revenue × gross margin % ÷ monthly churn
LTV ÷ CACLifetime margin you earn for each rupee spent winning the customer
Payback (months)CAC ÷ monthly gross margin per customer

Example (say these numbers): you spend 50,000 and get 10 new customers, so CAC is 5,000. Each pays 2,000 a month at 60% margin, so you keep 1,200 a month. Payback is 5,000 ÷ 1,200 ≈ 4.2 months. If 5% of customers leave each month, LTV = 1,200 ÷ 0.05 = 24,000, a ratio of 4.8.

What it meansBefore a gym owner buys new machines, they check which classes are full, which members quit and why. A growth audit is that check for your business: how it wins, converts and keeps customers. You rate each area Strong (works, is measured, can be repeated), Building (exists but is partial or unmeasured) or Gap (missing or broken). You finish with a scorecard and a ranked list of fixes.

Why it mattersIt shows where your next bit of effort pays off most. Without it, you chase a shiny new channel while the basics leak. It also gives you a starting point to compare against later.

How to use itGo through the 12 areas below. For each, collect evidence, not opinion: screenshots, reports, links, exports. Rate it, note the gap, and tie each fix to a card in this unit. Then rank the fixes with the method in . Repeat the audit regularly so progress shows. If a number doesn't exist, rate that area Gap and write "not measured yet".
