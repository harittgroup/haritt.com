Part 1 of 9

What performance marketing really is

You pay to bring people in, then judge the payment by what they do next.

Organic postCosts your time

If it fails, you lost very little.

vs
Paid adCosts money every day

You must be able to say what you got for it.

1
Suppose you put money behind an Instagram ad for HV Reset so that more students visit your site and join the beta. That is performance marketing. The name sounds technical, but the idea is plain: you pay to bring people to your product, and you judge the payment by what those people actually do afterwards, not by how many saw the ad.
2
This is different from posting on Instagram for free, which is organic marketing. An organic post costs you only time, so if it does not work you have lost very little and you can be a little casual. An ad costs money every single day it runs. That is exactly why you need to be able to say, at any moment, "I spent this much and I got this many people who did the thing I wanted." If you cannot say that sentence, you are not doing performance marketing, you are just spending money and hoping.
3
It also helps to know what paid ads cannot do. They cannot fix a product that students do not want, and they cannot rescue a confusing sign-up page. Ads only bring more people to whatever you already have. If what you have works, ads make it bigger and faster. If it does not work, ads make the problem bigger and more expensive. Keep that in mind, because the whole of Part 2 is about making sure there is something worth amplifying.

Are you ready?

Seven things must exist before paid ads make sense. Most are free to fix.

Most beginners start by opening an ad account. A better first step is to ask whether the ground is ready. Below are the things that must be in place before paid ads make sense. For each one, you will see why it matters, how to check it yourself, and where HV Verse stands today based on what we know.

AA product people get value fromAds bring people in. They cannot make them come back.

An ad can make someone try HV Reset once. It cannot make them come back. If students try the app and never return, every rupee you spend buys a visit that disappears. So before paying for new users, you need some evidence that the users you already have are finding value.

How to check: look at the students who joined in the beta and ask how many used it more than once, and what they said when you spoke to them. You do not need big numbers. Ten students who say "this helped me decide" is a much better foundation than a thousand who never opened it twice.

HV Verse today: the beta exists, but a clear picture of repeat use does not. This is the first thing to find out.

BA clear definition of a "good user"Without it, every campaign looks fine and none can be judged.

This is the point that people skip and later regret. When someone clicks your ad and fills the beta form, that is a sign-up. But a sign-up is not the same as a good user. A good user is a student who actually uses the app in the way that makes HV Verse worth building. If you never decide what that looks like, you have no way to tell a good campaign from a bad one. Every campaign will produce sign-ups, so every campaign will look fine, and you will keep spending without knowing whether any of it is useful.

Here is how it goes wrong. Say you run two ads. Ad one brings 20 sign-ups at ₹100 each. Ad two brings 10 sign-ups at ₹200 each. On the surface, ad one is twice as good. But if the 20 students from ad one never open the app and 6 of the 10 from ad two use it three times in their first week, ad two was far better. You only discover that if you decided beforehand what "good" means and you can count it.

What to do: write one sentence about behaviour, not about feelings. For example, "a good HV Reset user opens the app at least three times in the first week" or "completes their first decision." Pick something you can really count, because the whole point is to count it. Marketers call this chosen action the activation event, and the share of sign-ups who reach it is the activation rate.

HV Verse today: not defined. Until it is, you cannot judge any ad, so this blocks everything else.

CLabels on your links, so you can see where people came fromUTM tags tell you which post or ad sent each sign-up.

Right now, if ten students join the beta this week, you do not know whether they came from your Instagram bio, from a Reel, from a friend's WhatsApp message or from a Google search. They all arrive at the same form and look identical. A label fixes this. A label is a small piece of text added to the end of a web address, like ?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=beta-oct. The page looks exactly the same to the student, but your analytics can read the label and record that this visit came from Instagram, through a paid ad, as part of a campaign you named "beta-oct". These labels are called UTM tags.

Why this matters so much for ads: the ad platform will happily tell you how many clicks you got, but it only knows its own side. It cannot tell you how many of those people went on to become good users inside your product. Your own analytics can, but only if the visit arrived with a label. Without labels, the clicks you paid for and the users you gained become two separate stories that you cannot connect. With labels, you can say "the ₹2,000 I spent brought these 6 sign-ups, and 3 of them became good users."

What to do: use the UTM calculator on the Growth page to build one link for each place you share: bio, each post, each story, and later each ad. Keep the naming pattern the same every time, because "Instagram", "instagram" and "insta" are counted as three different sources.

HV Verse today: your links do not carry labels. That means even your free posts cannot be compared, so fix this first, and it costs nothing.

DA landing page that works on a phoneEvery slow second or extra field loses a click you already paid for.

The landing page is where a student arrives after clicking your ad, which in your case is the beta form. Nearly all your students will see it on a phone. Every second it takes to load, every extra field, every small button, every confusing line loses some of them. The painful part is that you have already paid for their click. A page that loses half of its visitors makes every ad twice as expensive, without you ever noticing.

How to check: open the form on your own phone using mobile data, not Wi-Fi. Time how long it takes to appear. Fill it in as a stranger would. Count the fields and ask whether you truly need each one. Then run the page through PageSpeed Insights, a free Google tool, and read what it says about mobile.

HV Verse today: unknown, so do this check now.

EEnough organic signal to learn fromCopy what already works for free. Wait for about 100 clicks of data.

Before paying, you should have seen some organic results: which posts students respond to, what words they use for their problem, which promise gets attention. This is valuable because your first ad should copy what already works for free. If you start ads with a guess, you are paying to learn what you could have learned from a free post.

There is also a numbers reason. A test with very few clicks proves nothing. Five sign-ups can easily become ten or two by pure luck, so you cannot tell if the ad was good. As a rough rule, you want at least about a hundred clicks before you start drawing conclusions, and more is better. That is part of why your decision to wait three months of organic data was a sensible one.

FMoney you can truly afford to lose, and a rule for when to stopDecide the limit while calm, and write it down.

The first campaign is a test. Think of the money as the price of an answer, not an investment you expect to get back. Decide the maximum amount, for example ₹2,000, and decide it while you are calm, not in the middle of a campaign. Many people raise the budget when results are poor because they feel they have already spent too much to quit. A written limit stops you from doing that.

GPrivacy and consent in orderNo pixel until consent and privacy text match. Labels are enough.

Ad platforms offer a small tracking snippet, called a pixel, that you place on your site so the platform can see who visited and show them ads again. It is powerful, but it collects personal data, so your privacy text and consent settings must match before you install one. For a first test you do not need it, since labelled links are enough to measure. Keep the pixel for later and do it properly.

The honest answer for HV Verse today: not ready, and that is fine. The three items that block you (a defined good user, labelled links, and a checked landing page) cost nothing and can be done in a few days. Spend the next weeks on them and on getting real students to use the beta. If your wait of three months comes to an end and these boxes are ticked, you are ready to start. If you are not ready even then, the right move is to improve the product and the free channels first, not to open an ad account.
Part 3 of 9

Planning the campaign

Five decisions, written on half a page, save most of the money.

1 Goalone result
2 Audienceone group
3 PlatformInstagram first
4 Messageone promise

Once you are ready, planning is where you save the most money. A good plan fits in half a page. It answers five questions in order.

GGoal: what exactly should this campaign achieve?One result, with a quality bar built in.

Pick one result. For HV Verse, it could be "get 15 students into the HV Reset beta who reach the good-user definition within a week." Notice that the goal includes the quality bar, not only sign-ups. Do not combine goals like "more followers, more sign-ups and more awareness". An ad platform can only optimise for one thing, and so can you.

AAudience: who should see it?Start with one audience you can already see in your data.

Start with the people your product is for. HV Reset is for students who feel unsure about what to do next with their studies or career. On Meta (Instagram and Facebook) you can narrow by age, location, language and interests. Beginners often make the audience too broad ("all students in India") or too tight (an odd mix of ten interests). A reasonable start is one audience: students in a particular age range and in a few cities or regions, with interests that match your topic. Keep it to one audience in the first test, so that if results are bad you know it was not because you split the money across five groups.

Judge the audience on what you already know, not on what sounds clever. Look at who engages with your organic posts and who joined the beta. If most are college students in their early twenties, start there.

PPlatform: where should the ad run?Instagram first. Your own form, not an instant form.

Choose the platform where your students already spend time and where you can send them to your goal. For HV Verse, Instagram is the natural first choice because it is your main platform and your students use it daily. The ads are managed through Meta Ads Manager, which also covers Facebook. Google search ads are weaker for you right now, since few students search for a brand-new app by name, and "HV" brings up electrical results.

One more decision on Meta: where should the click go? You can send people to your own beta form (your landing page), or use Meta's built-in instant form, where the student signs up without leaving Instagram. Instant forms usually get more sign-ups because there is less effort, but those sign-ups are often less serious, and the data sits inside Meta until you download it. For your first test, sending people to your own form is better, because you learn how your real page performs, and your labels work.

MMessage: what will the ad say?Problem in the headline, one promise, a button that matches.

An ad has three parts. The picture and the big line must stop a student who is scrolling quickly, so speak about their problem, for example "Stuck on what to do next?", not about your app. The short text carries one promise only, such as "Free beta for students. Get early access to HV Reset." The button, such as "Join the beta", should match what happens next so there are no surprises. Write two or three versions of the first line and use the one that is closest to how students already talk about their problem in your comments and messages.

Keep your claims honest. Do not promise results the app cannot give. It is both an ethics matter and a practical one, because misleading ads bring the wrong people, which will show up later as sign-ups that never become good users.

BBudget: how much, and for how long?About 100 clicks over 7 days, with three written limits.

Work backwards from what you need to learn. You want enough clicks to read the result, about a hundred as a minimum. If a click costs about ₹10 to ₹20 (this varies by audience and time, so treat it as a guess you will correct after day one), a hundred clicks cost ₹1,000 to ₹2,000. Run it for about seven days, because a single day can mislead and a week smooths out weekday and weekend differences. Write down three limits: the total you can spend, the day you stop, and the cost per good user at which you would call it a success. Decide the third one by asking what a good student is worth to you today, for example the time you would happily spend to get one such student by hand.

Setting up tracking

Three views of the same campaign. They must agree.

Meta Ads ManagerClicks and cost

Accurate about its own side only.

Your analyticsVisits and sign-ups

Needs labelled links and a form event.

The productGood-user action

Tied to the same sign-up.

1
Tracking is boring and it is the part that decides whether anything you learn is true. You need three views of the same campaign, and they must agree with each other.
2
The ad platform's view. Meta Ads Manager tells you how many people saw the ad (impressions), how many clicked, and what it cost. It is accurate about its own part, and nothing beyond.
3
Your own view. Your analytics (GA4 and your own tracker) should show how many visits arrived from the labelled ad link, how many finished the form, and later how many became good users. Check that the form completion is recorded as an event. If you cannot see an event for "beta form submitted", add it before you launch. A short test helps: click your own labelled link, submit the form with a test name, and confirm it appears in analytics with the right source and campaign name.
4
The product's view. The good-user action you defined in Part 2 must be recorded inside the product, tied to the same sign-up. This is what lets you say which campaign brought good users instead of just sign-ups.
5
Then compare. If Meta says 100 clicks and your analytics shows 40 visits from the label, something is wrong. Some difference is normal because people leave before the page loads, but a large gap usually means a broken link, a slow page or a tagging mistake, and you should fix that before spending more. Doing this check on day one is the cheapest quality control you have.
Part 5 of 9

Launching and the first week

Launch, then leave it alone. Check, do not edit.

Day 0test your own link
Day 1-2noisy, do not touch
Day 3-5daily check only
Day 7read results
1
When you launch, one rule matters more than any other: leave the campaign alone. The platform needs a few days to find the right people, and every change you make restarts part of that process. Looking at the numbers is fine, but do not edit the audience, the budget or the ad because day one looks poor. The first day is almost always noisy.
2
What you should do is a short check each day. Confirm the ad is running and has not been rejected by the platform. Confirm the spend is within your limit. Spot-check that new sign-ups with the campaign label are appearing in your own analytics. If something is broken, for example the link leads to an error page, that is the one reason to stop and fix it immediately.
3
Also write down your prediction before you see results, such as "I expect about 5 percent of clicks to sign up and a cost of about ₹400 per sign-up." Predictions make you learn faster, because you will see where your picture of the market was wrong.

At the end of the week, read the numbers in the order students experienced them, because that order shows where people dropped away. Each number is a step in a chain, and each step answers a different question.

ImpressionsTimes the ad was shownWhether the platform could find people to show it to

1
A worked example, with made-up numbers: you spend ₹2,000. The ad is shown 25,000 times and gets 125 clicks, so the click rate is 0.5 percent and each click costs ₹16. Your analytics records 110 visits from the label, and 6 students submit the form, a sign-up rate of about 5.5 percent and ₹333 per sign-up. Two of those six become good users, so activation is 33 percent and the cost per good user is ₹1,000. Whether ₹1,000 per good user is acceptable depends on what such a student is worth to you, which is the reason you decided that threshold before launching.
2
Notice that the final cost came from three multiplied weaknesses and strengths: the click cost, the sign-up rate and the activation rate. If any one of them is poor, the final cost is high. That is exactly why diagnosis, in the next part, works.

A failed campaign is useful if you can find out why. The trap is guessing, usually "the landing page must be bad". A failure can come from six different places, and each one leaves different marks in the numbers. Work through them in this order, from the cheapest to check to the hardest, and stop when you find a clear culprit.

1Is the tracking telling the truth?Platform clicks vs your visits. Test your own form.

Before blaming anything, check that your numbers are right. Compare the ad platform's clicks with the visits in your analytics. If the platform says 125 clicks and you see 30 visits, your problem is not the ad. People may be leaving before the page loads, the link may be broken on some phones, or your label is missing on part of the traffic. Also submit a test form yourself and confirm it is recorded. If the tracking is wrong, fix it and treat this week's conclusions as unreliable.

2Is the audience wrong?Low click rate, and breakdowns show the wrong people.

Look at: impressions and click rate. If the ad is shown to many people but almost nobody clicks (a very low click rate while the ad itself seems strong), the platform is showing it to people who do not care. Also check who actually saw it, in the breakdown by age, gender and location in Ads Manager. If a lot of the spend went to a group you did not intend, such as much older people, the audience is the issue.

Pattern: low click rate across every version of the ad points to the audience. If different ads get very different click rates to the same audience, the problem is the ad rather than the audience.

3Is the ad itself weak?Right people, but they scroll past.

Look at: click rate and cost per click for each ad version. A weak first line or picture shows as people scrolling past, so impressions are fine and clicks are low. An ad that is dramatic but vague may get lots of clicks from the curious who then do nothing, and that shows in the next step.

Pattern: low click rate with good targeting means the message does not stop the scroll. Compare two or three first lines, and read the comments under the ad. Students often tell you what they understood.

4Is the promise different from what the page delivers?Good clicks, visitors leave in seconds.

Look at: the gap between clicks and sign-ups, plus how long visitors stay. If the click rate is healthy but visitors leave within a few seconds, the ad made a promise that the page did not continue. For example, the ad says "free early access" and the page opens with a long paragraph about the company. Students feel misled and leave. This is called a message mismatch.

Pattern: many clicks, very short visits, very few sign-ups.

5Is the landing page losing people?They stay and start, but do not finish the form.

Look at: where inside the page people drop off. If visitors stay a while, scroll and start the form but do not finish it, the form is the problem: too many fields, a confusing question, a button that does not work well on some phones, or slow loading. Watch a few real sessions on your own phone and in a free tool like Microsoft Clarity if you have added it.

Pattern: a decent click rate and engaged visitors but a low sign-up rate, with started forms that are not completed.

6Is the offer or the product itself the issue?Sign-ups are fine, but they never become good users.

Look at: activation. If the first five steps look healthy, with good clicks, steady visits and a good sign-up rate, but students who join never reach your good-user action, the real issue is deeper. Either the ad attracted people who are not really your audience, or the product's first experience does not deliver what the ad promised. Speak to five of the students who signed up and never came back. Ask what they expected and what happened. This is where paid ads stop being the problem and the product becomes the thing to fix, which is a valuable thing to learn for the price of ₹2,000.

Pattern: many sign-ups, low activation, and the same reasons repeated in your conversations.

Part 8 of 9

Fixing it and proving it

Change one thing. Predict. Rerun. Compare. Log.

GuessOne sentence, with a number
Change one thingOnly the suspect
RerunSame audience, budget, length
CompareAgainst the earlier week
LogBelief, change, result, decision
1
Once you have a likely culprit, change only that thing. This is the most important discipline of the whole process. If you change the ad, the page and the audience together, and results improve, you will not know which change helped, so you cannot repeat it. If they get worse, you will not know which hurt.
2
Write your guess in one sentence before you change anything: "I believe the form loses students because it has six fields. If I cut it to three, the sign-up rate will go from about 5 percent to about 8 percent." Make the change, run it again with the same audience and the same budget for the same length of time, and compare against the earlier week. If the sign-up rate moves as predicted, you were right and you keep the change. If it does not, your guess was wrong, and that is also useful: cross that one off and move to the next likely cause on your list.
3
Keep a simple test log with these columns: date, what you believed, what you changed, the result and the decision. After a few rounds, this log becomes your own marketing knowledge about your students, which no course can give you.
Part 9 of 9

Scale, repeat or stop

Grow slowly, repeat with one change, or stop on purpose.

Cost within your limitScale slowly

About a fifth more every few days. Step back if cost rises past the limit.

Over the limit, fixableFix and retest

Change one thing and run the test again.

Over the limit, productStop the ads

Improve the first experience. Return later.

1
When the cost per good user is within the limit you set, do not jump to a large budget. Increase slowly, for example by about a fifth every few days, and watch whether the cost per good user holds. Results often get worse as you widen the audience, because the first people reached were the easiest ones. If the cost rises beyond your limit, step back to the last level that worked.
2
If after two or three honest rounds the cost per good user is still far above your limit and the cause is the product, stop the ads. Put the money and attention into improving the first experience, and return to ads later. Stopping is not failure here. It is the result of the test, and it saved you from spending a larger amount on something that was not ready.
3
Once a campaign works, repeat it with a new audience or a new message, again changing one thing at a time. Over months, this is how you learn what your students respond to, and it is the skill that matters most in a marketing career.
