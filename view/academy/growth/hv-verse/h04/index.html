1
When someone types "free job application tracker" into Google and your page appears, that visit is called organic search traffic. Search engine optimisation (SEO) is the work of making your pages easy for Google to find and worth showing. It has three parts. Crawling is bots visiting your pages. Indexing is Google storing and understanding them. Ranking is the order of results. People who search are already looking, so they are ready to act, and a good page can bring them for years without a per-click cost. The catch is that it starts slowly, so if you wait until you are desperate for students, you will wait months.
2
Two related ideas sit in this lesson. PR, short for public relations, means earning mentions in other places, and a backlink is a link from another website to yours, which search engines treat a bit like a vote of trust. Local search is about being found by place, such as "near me", through a Google Business Profile. I cover local briefly at the end, because it does not fit an online product.
Do you need this now? Partly. The setup (Google Search Console, a sitemap, plain page titles, a few directory listings) is free and should start this week, because it takes a while for Google to learn a new site and you lose nothing by starting. The content and ranking work is for later. With a two or three week old name and no content yet, you should not expect search to bring students soon. Your first students will come from groups, social posts and sharing.

The sections below walk through what must be ready, how to set up Search Console step by step, how to write pages that match what students type, how to read the numbers, and what to do when nothing shows up.

Google Search Console is Google's free tool that shows how your site appears in search. It tells you which searches showed you, how many people clicked, and which pages Google has stored. It is the main tool of this lesson. Menu names and layouts change from time to time, so read the steps as a guide and look for the nearest label.

1Add the propertyChoose Domain, not URL prefix, if you can.

Go to Search Console and sign in with the Google account you want to own the data. Click the property selector at the top left, then Add property. Choose Domain and type thehvverse.com. A Domain property covers every address under your domain, with or without www, which suits a site that has several app pages.

2Prove ownershipA short text value in your DNS.

Google shows a TXT record. Open your Cloudflare dashboard, choose the thehvverse.com domain, open DNS, then Records, then Add record. Choose type TXT, put @ as the name, paste the value and save. Return to Search Console and click Verify. If it says not found, wait a little and try again, since DNS changes take time to spread. If you prefer, the URL prefix option offers an HTML file or a meta tag instead.

3Send the sitemapA list of pages you want found.

In the left menu open Indexing, then Sitemaps. Type the sitemap address of your site (check it first by opening it in a browser) and click Submit. The status should change to Success after some time. Your files show an older version of the site had 12 addresses, so confirm the live one lists the current pages.

4Inspect the key pagesAsk Google to look at them.

Paste the full address of your home page into the search bar at the top, the URL Inspection tool. If it says the page is not on Google, click Request indexing. Do this for the home page and each app page. It is a request, not a promise, and it does not speed up ranking. Do not request indexing for the beta page, because it is marked noindex on purpose.

5Add Bing tooA free second search tool.

In Bing Webmaster Tools, sign in and add your site. It offers an option to import from Google Search Console, which saves repeating the steps. Bing is small for students but takes minutes.

Everything in search begins with search intent, which means what the person wants when they type. Some want to learn (how to track job applications), some want a tool (free job application tracker), some want to compare (best planner apps for students), and some are looking for you by name. Each type needs a different page. The table below matches the types to your apps. The queries are examples of the kind of phrase, not data about volume, since no such numbers exist in your files.

HHow to find the wordsUse real questions, not guesses.

Start with questions students asked you in chats and on the beta form. Then type the beginning of a phrase into Google and read the suggestions it offers below the box. Later, Search Console will show the exact searches that brought people, which beats any guess. Google Keyword Planner is another free option, though it needs a Google Ads account. Avoid paid keyword tools for now.

WWrite one page per intentTitle, first screen, proof.

Give each page a title that says what it does in plain words, a first screen that answers the search at once, and a real screenshot of the app. Do not stuff the page with repeated phrases. Link between the home page and each app page, so Google finds them easily.

DDirectories and a few honest linksA free route for free apps.

A directory lists products by category, and a backlink from a relevant one tells Google you exist. Suitable places for free apps include AlternativeTo, Product Hunt and student tool lists, plus college newsletters that mention the Student Beta. Write a one-line and a three-line description once and reuse them everywhere, with the same name each time. Never buy links or swap them in bulk, since that breaks Google's rules and can sink your rankings. The 14 October study lists LinkedIn, Product Hunt, AlternativeTo, Crunchbase and Wellfound as to-dos, but their status cannot be verified.

Open Search Console, then Performance, then Search results. The report shows four numbers for a date range you choose. The help page says the default is the last three months. Data starts the day you verify, so there is no history, and the first rows can take days to appear. Look once a week and use a 28 day range, because search moves slowly and a single day tells you nothing.

Where: the second tile above the chart. Reading it: compare with your own last week, not with anyone else. A brand new site often sits near zero for a few weeks, and that is normal. Once it rises, open the Queries tab and ask if these are the searches you wanted.

Problem pattern: still zero after a month. Action: run URL inspection on the home page and read the Page indexing report, as in part 6.

BClicks and CTR: do people choose youClick through rate is clicks over impressions.

Reading it: in the made-up screen, 6 clicks from 120 impressions is 5 percent. CTR depends on position, so a page at position 3 should do much better than a page at position 38. Judge CTR only for rows with enough impressions.

Problem pattern: high impressions, high position, tiny CTR. Action: your title or description does not say what the app does. Rewrite it, note the date, and look again after two weeks.

CAverage position: where you sitLower is better.

Reading it: position 3 is near the top of page one, 10 is the bottom of page one, and 19 means page two. The chart value covers your whole site, while the table value is for that one query or page. The 14 October study found HV Reset at rank 4 for its name with electric-vehicle pages above it.

Problem pattern: a plain phrase like "free job application tracker" with many impressions but position 38. Action: this is a content gap, not a title gap. Write one good page for that phrase and wait weeks.

DQueries, Pages, Countries, DevicesGroup the same numbers four ways.

Queries show the words. Pages show which of your pages won. Countries matter because your audience is Indian students: many impressions from other countries mean your words are too general. Devices show phone against desktop, and students should be mostly on phones.

Click a row to filter, and use Compare for two date ranges.

IIndexed and not indexedNot every page must be indexed.

Under Indexing, the Pages report is the Page indexing report. It splits your known addresses into Indexed and Not indexed, then lists reasons in the table called Why pages aren't indexed. Google says not indexed is not always bad. A page that redirects, a duplicate that points to the main page, or a page you marked noindex on purpose, such as the Beta page, are fine. Worry when a key page sits there: the home page, an app page. Click the reason, read the sample addresses, fix the cause, then press Validate fix. A reason that starts with Discovered, currently not indexed means Google knows the address but has not fetched it yet, so improve the page and its internal links and wait.

HHow to tell it workedClicks are not the goal.

The real goal is students who open and use an app, not clicks. So compare organic visits with first actions in your dashboard. Because of the suspected counting bug in returning visitors, read retention numbers with care. The study on 14 October expected brand name results in one to three weeks and long phrases only after six months or more of content and links. That is the study's reading, not a promise.

Search fails more quietly than ads. There is no bill and no flat line, just a slow absence. So you need a clear order of checks.

1Can Google reach it?Verify, sitemap, robots.

Make sure the property is verified, the sitemap shows Success and the robots file does not block the pages. Also check the page is not set to noindex by mistake. Only the beta page should be.

2Is it indexed?Use URL Inspection and the Pages report.

If the page is not on Google, read the reason. Common causes are a redirect loop, a duplicate page, or a page that Google judged thin. Fix the reason, then request indexing once.

3Does the title match the want?Rewrite with plain words.

Pair the app name with what it does. Change one page at a time, and note the date, so you can tell later whether the change helped.

4Is there enough useful content?One strong page beats five thin ones.

Add a short guide with real screens and an honest answer to the search. Wait several weeks, then compare impressions on that page.

5Are there a few trusted links?Directories and college mentions.

Submit to two relevant directories with the same text. Check the Links report in a month. Stop if rankings do not move after a few months of honest work, and put the time into social groups instead.

Local search: why it does not fit, and when to revisit

An online product has no place of business. Use place-based routes instead.

Google Business ProfileFor businesses with a place

Map listing, reviews, calls, directions.

vs
HV VerseAn online product

Students find it in groups and on phones, not by a map pin.

Local search means a search with a place in mind, such as "web designer near me". The free tool for it is the Google Business Profile, a verified listing with a name, address, hours, photos and reviews. It suits a shop or a service that people visit or that serves an area. HV Verse is an online product with no place of business, so a profile would have no real address to show and Google expects a profile to belong to a real business with a way for customers to reach it. That tool suits a business that has a place or a service area, not your apps.

AWhy you skip itNo place, no map pin.

Google ranks local results on relevance, distance and prominence. Your apps have no distance, so there is nothing for a profile to rank on. Spend the effort elsewhere.

BWhat works insteadPlace-based routes through colleges.

Students gather in colleges and coaching centres. Posters with a tagged QR code in two chosen colleges are the closest thing to local marketing for you. Give each poster its own utm_content value so you can tell which one brought visits.

Revisit search in two moments. First, a month after verifying Search Console, to read the first real queries. Second, after three months of data, when you can decide whether to invest in guide pages for each app. If impressions are growing by then, search is earning its place. If they are flat, keep the setup alive but give your time to groups and social posting.
