Part 1 of 6

What testing means when your traffic is small

A test is a fair comparison of a written guess. For HV Verse today that mostly means big changes and listening.

A guessChange it and hope

You cannot tell what worked, and you repeat mistakes.

vs
A testWrite it, compare fairly, log it

You learn something even when the idea fails.

1
Imagine you change the first screen of HV Reset on Monday and sign-ups rise on Friday. Did the change work? Maybe. Or maybe a friend shared your link, or exams ended and students had time. A test is a way of answering that fairly. You write down a guess before you change anything, you decide which single number will judge it, and you compare against something that ran at the same time or against a clear earlier baseline. Then you record what happened, even if nothing did.
2
The most famous kind is an A/B test. You show version A (the current one, called the control) to half your visitors and version B (the changed one, the variant) to the other half, picked at random, and compare one number. Because the split is random, any gap came from your change and not from the week. That sounds ideal, but it needs a lot of visitors, which is the catch for HV Verse.
Do you need this now? Not a true A/B test. Your site launched on 1 October and no HV Verse traffic figure is on file. The only traffic on record, from the Developers tab on 13 October, was 293 summed visitor-days in 30 days, far too few for statistical tests. What you do need now is the habit: write hypotheses, score ideas so the cheap fixes come first, keep a learning log, and test only big changes using the Student Beta groups and honest before-and-after comparisons.

So this lesson is a plan for testing properly at your size, and a map for when your traffic grows. The skill transfers: the same written guess, the same log, the same fair comparison, only with different tools later.

Testing on top of broken measurement gives you confident wrong answers, which is worse than guessing. Check these before you run anything.

AThe counting worksIf the counter lies, every test result lies.

Your admin dashboard shows weekly active users as 0 and Returning as 0, which cannot be right. Until that is fixed, any test judged on return visits is meaningless. A test judged on a simple event such as a beta application can still be read, but test the count on your own device first.

BYou know what a good result isYou need one number that says the change helped.

The number that decides a test is the primary metric. For HV Reset it could be the share of visitors who finish a first task. For HV Hunt, the share who add a first job. For HV Assess, the share who complete the test. The code already fires events for these, but you have not agreed the definitions. Agree them first, then every test has something to be judged on.

Run your funnel for a few full weeks and write down the numbers at each step. That is your baseline. Your own plan said to set targets after four weeks, and that is the same idea. Without a baseline you cannot tell a gain from normal wobble.

A hypothesis is a guess written so that it can turn out wrong. The format that works is: "Because we saw X, we believe changing Y for group Z will improve metric M, which we will see in the number N." The evidence part matters. A hypothesis with no evidence behind it is just an idea.

AA first hypothesis for HV VerseReal gap from your own files, so it is not invented.

Your Beta form stores no source, so you cannot tell which poster or group produced an application. A hypothesis: "Because applications carry no source, we believe adding a one-question field, How did you hear about us, will let us see which group works, and we will check that most new applications have an answer." It is more a measurement fix than a test, but it is the right first entry because it makes later tests possible.

PPrimary metric, guardrail and decision ruleSet these before you start, not after you see results.

The primary metric decides the test. A guardrail is a number that must not get worse. For example, if you shorten HV Reset's first screen to raise task completion, a guardrail could be the share who say it confused them in feedback. A decision rule says in advance what you will do: "Keep the change if the first-task rate is higher and no guardrail drops. Otherwise revert."

WWhere to keep the logFree, simple, and you will actually open it.

Create a Google Sheet with the columns in the picture, one row per test. Failed tests deserve rows too, since a log of only wins teaches you nothing and invites you to repeat the same dead ideas six months later. Link each row to the matching entry in your change log so the screenshot and the result sit together.

Rule of thumb for the whole thing: change one idea at a time, leave the test alone while it runs, and write the result even if the result is "unclear".

How to read the log. The Before and After columns are the heart of it. Write the count and the group size in the same cell, such as 6 of 40, never only a percent. Read down the Decision column every month: rows with no decision are tests you forgot to finish. Rows that say unclear several times in a row mean your changes are too small for your traffic, so make the next one bolder. Rows that never reach After mean the test was never run, which is a planning problem and not a result.

You will always have more ideas than time. Without a method, the newest idea or the loudest opinion wins. ICE scoring gives each idea three marks from 1 to 10: Impact (how much it could move your primary metric), Confidence (how sure you are, based on evidence rather than hope) and Ease (how little effort it takes). The ICE score is the average of the three. Pick the highest, do it, and log it.

Your Growth page already holds ten ideas drawn from the gaps in your files. Most are foundation fixes: define activation, fix the Returning bug, clean the Beta posters, add a source field, tag scorecard shares, and update privacy text. In the picture, the scores are made up to show how the ranking looks. Use the tool to put in your own marks.

SScoring honestlyEvidence beats opinion.

Confidence is where people cheat. Give a high mark only if you have something behind it: a student's comment, a funnel step with a visible drop, a repeat complaint in the Beta reports. With nothing measured yet, confidence is low across the board, and that is fine. It tells you the first job is to gather evidence.

WWhen to override the scoreScores guide your judgement, they do not replace it.

If a low-scoring idea blocks everything else, such as fixing the counter, do it first. Also watch ease: a cheap fix that takes an afternoon often beats a clever redesign that takes weeks. Once you have real reach numbers per idea, you can move to RICE, which adds Reach and divides by Effort, but that is for later.

Write the top three in your learning log with the date. Then do them one at a time.

How to read the sheet. The ICE column is a ranking tool, not a measurement. Two ideas with scores within about one point are a tie, so pick the easier one. A high Impact with Confidence under 4 means you are hoping, not knowing, and the first task is to gather evidence, such as asking five testers. An idea that scores high only because Ease is 10 is a quick fix, which is fine, but do not call it your strategy. Re-score every month, because a finished test changes the Confidence of its neighbours.

The honest truth is that most small sites cannot run classic A/B tests. A rough rule from the testing card says the visitors needed per version is about 16 times the variation in the metric, divided by the square of the smallest change you care about. Worked example with made-up numbers: if 5 in 100 visitors sign up and you want to detect a rise of 1 point, you would need about 7,600 visitors per version. HV Verse is nowhere near that on a single page, so a tiny button change cannot be tested.

What to do instead, in this order:

  1. Test big changes. A large change needs far fewer visitors to show. Change the whole offer or first screen, not a colour.
  2. Measure something closer. A click on the Beta form happens more often than a finished application, so you learn sooner.
  3. Go qualitative. About five people usually reveal most usability problems in one round. Your Beta reports, ideas and survey answers are exactly this. Read them every week and group the top three issues.
  4. Use before and after carefully. Write down everything else that changed that week.

YYour best A/B setup todayPoster A in one group, poster B in another.

The Student Beta groups are a natural place for a simple comparison. Post poster A in one student group and poster B in a similar group, with the same style of labelled link, and compare applications and visits to the Beta page by source. Both must go out at the same time to similar audiences. Two groups of different size or different colleges are not equal, so treat the result as a hint, not proof.

Before you start, fix the rules: how long it runs (whole weeks), what you will not touch, and what result you will act on. Do not stop early because one side looks ahead after two days. That is called peeking, and early gaps often vanish.

How to read the Campaigns panel. Sessions are visits, engaged is the share that stayed 10 seconds, saw two pages or did an event, and opened an app is the real step. Compare posters by the share of sessions that opened an app, never by raw sessions, because one group may simply be bigger. If sessions are high and engaged is low, the poster promised something the page did not show. If a big share lands under Direct, your links were not tagged or came from an app that hides the referrer, so the test is not fair. Compare each poster with your own earlier week, and write the group sizes in the log.

Read the result and decide: ship, drop or retest

Win, lose or unclear are all answers. Write the learning and pick the next idea.

When the time you set is over, open the log row and fill the Result and Decision columns before you form any opinion. Look at the actual counts, not only percentages. A rise from 2 of 10 to 4 of 10 is a 100 per cent increase and tells you almost nothing. Always write both the counts and the size of the groups.

1Check the test itselfDid something go wrong before the result?

Was the split what you planned? Did the link work for both versions? Did tracking fire for both? If a poster link carried no label, that arm is invisible, and the test is void.

2If it failed, find the layerIs it the audience, the ad, the page, the offer or tracking?

If few people clicked, the poster or message is the issue. If many clicked but few applied, look at the form or page. If people applied but never used the app, it is the product or the first experience. If nothing can be seen at all, it is tracking. Use your funnel panels to see which step lost them.

3Fix and retestOne new test per lesson learned.

Make one change aimed at that layer, write a new hypothesis, and run again. Stop an idea after two honest tries if it gives nothing. Scale a winner only after it holds for a couple of weeks. Most importantly, add the learning in plain words, such as "students ignored the second poster because the benefit was in small text", so you never pay for the same lesson twice.

Revisit proper A/B testing when you have a steady flow of visitors to one page and a clear primary metric. Until then, fewer, bigger, well-logged bets will teach you more.
