1
Conversion rate optimisation (CRO) is a long name for a simple habit: take the page people land on, and make it easier for more of them to do the one thing you want. A shop owner does the same when she moves the popular item near the door. For HV Verse, the page that matters most right now is the Student Beta page at thehvverse.com/beta/. Posters, WhatsApp messages and creator posts all point to it, so every earlier effort is wasted if this page is confusing, slow or asks too much.
2
A landing page is a page built for one visitor type and one action. That is different from your home page, which has to serve everyone. A student who scans a poster has one question: what is this and is it for me? The page should answer it in the first screen on a phone, show a single button, and then the form, which is the set of questions they fill in, should feel short and safe. The words on the page (the copy), the proof it gives (trust) and how fast it opens (speed) all decide whether they carry on.
3
Your own information says the live pages cannot be checked from the files, and the Beta page is no exception. So the first step of this lesson is to look at the real page yourself, on a phone, as a stranger.
Do you need this now? Yes. The Beta campaign is your main organic route, and this page and form are where it either works or leaks. The work is free and mostly editing. What you should not do is polish the design or run fine A/B tests: traffic is too small for that, so fix the obvious problems and move on.

Here is what your files show. The form asks for name, profession, why they want to join, and a WhatsApp number or an email (at least one). The admin view also shows a Discord ID. It stores no source, so you cannot tell which poster brought someone in. The consent wording is not visible. No testimonials, ratings, user counts or press appear anywhere, and there are no page speed or accessibility scores on file. One technical risk: the stored rules for the applications database must be re-pasted from the rules page in the admin, or submissions fail, and that cannot be confirmed from files.

FFive quick testsCheck readiness before sending traffic

Run all five before the next poster or message goes out. They take under an hour. If any fails, fix it first, because sending people to a broken page costs you the first impression.

1The real form testSubmit it yourself.

Fill the form on your own phone with a test entry and then find it on the Signups page in admin. If it does not appear, nothing else matters yet.

2Mobile data, cheap phoneOpen it on a bad connection.

Use mobile data, not Wi-Fi, ideally on a low-end Android. Note how long before you can read the headline and press the button.

3Without any contextRead it as a stranger.

Ask a friend who has never heard of HV Verse to say, after five seconds, what it is and who it is for.

4Names and countdownsLook for old text.

Check for old names such as HV World, Vault or Test, an old launch countdown, and any mention of another brand in the page description.

5Matches what you actually doCheck the privacy line.

The page should say how details are used, and the privacy page should match what is loaded, including Google Analytics.

Part 3 of 6

Planning the page: promise, copy, proof and fields

Decide what the page says before you touch a design.

PromiseWhat a student gets

One line, in the student's words.

ProofOnly what is real

Privacy, free access, honest perks.

AskThe fewest fields

Name and one contact.

Start with the promise, because every other choice follows from it. Your poster lines are a good base: "Your next project shouldn't run on dummy data" and "What you do. What you take away." The page should repeat the same promise in the first heading, so the student feels they landed in the right place. This is called message match, and it is one of the cheapest fixes there is. If the poster says one thing and the page opens with another, people leave in seconds.

PPlain, specific, honestCopy that works for students

Say who it is for (students and freshers), what they do (test real apps and report what is broken), what they get (points, and perks that are clearly marked as not guaranteed), and how long it takes. Pick one name and one duration for the whole campaign. Use one button with a verb, such as "Apply for the Beta". Avoid words that promise more than you can give, and never add a number that is not real.

WWhat you can honestly showTrust without invented proof

There are no testimonials or user counts, so do not make any. Show what is true: the apps are free, HV Assess answers stay in the browser unless a scorecard is saved, and your data is yours. Ask five testers for a one-line quote with permission, and add it when you have it. Show a count of testers only when the number is real and you can check it.

EEvery extra field loses peopleForm fields: keep the minimum

Name and one contact (WhatsApp or email) is enough to start. Profession helps you learn who applies. Keep the why question optional if you can, or short. Add a hidden source field that saves utm_source and utm_content from the link, so each application carries its origin. Add one sentence about how answers are used. Because your audience includes 16 and 17 year olds, collect only what you need.

DDo not split too earlyOne page, or one per audience

Your files raise the idea of one landing page for students and another for institutes. For now, keep one Beta page for students, because the institute route is only a plan and has no capture at all. Add a second page only when real institute conversations start. Until then a second page just doubles the checking you have to do.

SStudents use cheap phonesSpeed matters more than polish

A page that opens in two seconds on mobile data beats a beautiful page that opens in eight. Keep images small, load nothing you do not need, and avoid big videos at the top.

Hands-on: checking speed and fixing the form

Use PageSpeed Insights for speed, then add the source field and the form events.

1Address boxPaste https://thehvverse.com/beta/ and press Analyze.
2Mobile tabRead this one first, since students use phones.
3Performance scoreA number from 0 to 100. Use it to compare before and after.
4Core Web VitalsLoad, response and layout stability measures.
5OpportunitiesA list of the biggest fixes, such as large images.

PageSpeed Insights is a free Google tool that measures how fast a public page loads and lists what slows it down. Run it on the Beta page, the home page and each app page, and write the scores in your audit. Do not chase 100. Look for the largest items in the list, usually a heavy image or a script, and fix the top one or two. Then run it again and note the change. The tool's wording can change, but the idea stays the same.

ASave where each applicant came fromHidden source field.

Ask whoever edits the form to read utm_source, utm_medium, utm_campaign and utm_content from the address when the page opens, and save them with the application record. Also accept the old src value, so existing poster links still count. Then the Signups page can show a source beside every name.

BUnder the buttonOne consent line.

Add a single sentence, for example that the answers are used to run the Beta and reply to applicants, and match it to the privacy page. Your wording must be checked by you.

CSee where people stopForm events.

Your counter already records a form start event on some sites. Make sure the Beta page records three things: page view, form started (first click into a field) and application sent. Without them you cannot see where people drop.

DBefore every campaignReal device test.

Submit the form on a cheap phone, on mobile data, in a messaging app's own browser (the one that opens when someone taps a link inside WhatsApp or Instagram). Many students will arrive that way.

Part 5 of 6

Reading the results: did the page work?

Follow the chain from visit to first use, and compare against your own earlier version.

200tagged visits to /beta/
70clicked into a field
30applications
20status joined
12activation event

Made-up example to show the method. These are not real numbers.

Because traffic is small, read the chain in plain counts and in simple shares. Say 200 tagged visits led to 70 who started the form and 30 who sent it (all made up). The share that started is 35 in 100, and the share that finished after starting is 30 of 70. These two ratios tell different stories. If few people start, the top of the page is weak: the promise, the button or the speed. If many start but few finish, the form is the problem: too many fields, an unclear contact box, an error on submit, or a worry about privacy.

Where do the numbers live? Visits by tag are in your admin dashboard's channels and campaigns tables. Applications are on the Signups page, with status new, contacted, joined or closed. First use comes from the activation events. Put the three side by side for each group you reached, for the same dates. Remember that GA4, after lesson 10, will give a second view of the same visits, and the two tools will not match exactly, so use one for decisions.

FFive short conversationsTalk to the people behind the numbers

Numbers show where people stop, not why. After each group, message three or four applicants and ask what they expected, what confused them, and what nearly stopped them. Write the answers down in the learning log. In a campaign this small, five honest replies often explain more than a week of counts, and they give you the words to use in the next version of the headline.

TThree checksHow to tell it worked

The share who start the form rises after a change. Applicants say the page made sense when you talk to them. And the people who join actually use an app. A rise in applications with no rise in use means you attracted the wrong people or promised too much.

BBe careful with ratesWhat small numbers allow

With under a few hundred visits, one person changes a rate. Compare big changes, run the same message in several groups, and treat two weeks of before-and-after as a hint, not proof.

When it fails: finding the cause and fixing it

Find the weakest step, change one thing, then check the same step again.

What do your numbers show?

Tap what you see. The likely culprit shows here.
Most likelyMessage or link

Is the link clear and tagged? Did the group see it?

Most likelyMessage mismatch or speed

Does the headline match the poster? Run PageSpeed.

Most likelyPage or trust

One clear button? A reason to believe? Any proof?

Most likelyThe form

Cut fields, fix errors, test on a phone.

Most likelyFollow-up

How quickly do you reply? Is the next step clear?

Most likelyProduct or expectations

Talk to five of them about what they expected.

Failure here usually has one of five causes: the message, the speed, the page, the form or the follow-up. The table above links each pattern to its likely cause, and the check beside it tells you what to open first. Resist the urge to rewrite everything. Pick the earliest step in the chain that looks weak.

1If I change X, Y will improveWrite the guess down.

For example: if I cut the form to name and one contact, more people who start will finish, because the form is the weakest step. A guess you wrote down can be judged later.

2Then send to a comparable groupChange one thing.

Use a new content tag so the two versions are separate in the dashboard. Poster A in one college and poster B in another is a simple fair test.

3Compare the same ratioCheck the same step.

Look at the same share you were worried about. If it moved clearly, keep the change. If not, undo it and try the next idea. Add each result to your learning log.

4Be honest with the numbersScale or stop.

If the page converts well but few people arrive, go back to community and groups. If many arrive but few apply even after fixes, the offer may be the issue, not the page, so talk to the students.
