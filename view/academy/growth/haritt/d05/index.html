What a stranger needsEvidence someone like them was helped

A named result, a short quote, a real project.

1
Think about the owner of a small shop or clinic who lands on developers.haritt.com. They have never heard of Haritt Developers. They are about to share a budget and a business problem with a stranger, and the site gives them very little to check. From your files: the site lists 7 services and custom quotes, but there are no client case studies, no testimonials and no client logos. The work shown is in-house, including HV Verse. Nothing is wrong with that, it is simply early. The point is that for a studio selling a project worth tens of thousands of rupees, trust decides most enquiries, and trust is built from proof.
2
This lesson covers four ideas that all work on the same problem. Content is what you publish (answers, build stories, guides). Public presence, which marketers call PR (public relations), is where others mention or list you: directories, profiles, links from other sites. Trust is the sum of signals that you are real and reliable. Social media is where you show the content to people who might remember you. Taught through your business, they become one job: show that you can do the work, in places where your future clients already look.
3
A word on terms. A backlink is a link to your site from another site, and search engines treat a few good ones as a sign that you are real. A case study is a short honest write-up of one project: the problem, what you built, and what changed. A testimonial is a short quote from a client. Social proof just means evidence that other people have used and valued you.
Do you need this now? Yes, and it comes before any paid effort. Your files show no client proof, the founding year differs across the site, your story and Justdial, and the plan is organic only for now. Fixing these costs time, not money, and every later channel (referrals, email, search ads) performs better when a visitor can check you out. Start with one honest case study about the HV Verse build, and fix the founding year.

Before writing anything, check the ground. These are the things that must be in place so that the work you publish actually builds trust instead of creating doubts. For each item you will see why it matters, how to check it, and where Haritt Developers stands from the files.

AOne founding year everywhereVisitors who compare sources trust you less.

About says "Founded in 2026", your own story is a start in 2020 with a relaunch in 2026, and Justdial says "Established 2018, Bharatpur". A careful buyer who sees three versions wonders what else is loose.

How to check: search your own name, open the About page, the Justdial listing and the LinkedIn page, and write the year each one shows. Fix: decide one honest line, for example one that mentions the 2026 relaunch if that is the truth you want to tell, and then change every place to match. Only the founders can pick the line, so this is a decision for you, not a guess.

BA real project you may talk aboutNo approved client work yet.

The site already promises that client case studies will be added only with the client's approval. Keep that promise. Until a client agrees, the project you can talk about freely is your own: HV Verse, built and launched on 1 Oct 2026.

How to check: list every project you have delivered and write beside each one whether the client has said yes to being named. If the column is empty, the own-build story is your first piece, and the next client gets asked for approval at the start of the project, not at the end.

CA place to publish and a next stepContent with no next step is wasted.

Every piece needs a home on your site and a clear action at the bottom, usually "Start a project" or "Discuss your project". The site has a 10-question FAQ, a sitemap and keyword titles, so the structure exists.

How to check: open a service page on your phone and see whether a visitor can reach the enquiry form in one tap. The only article on haritt.com today is a career guide meant for another audience, so business-owner content has no home yet.

DLinks you can measureOtherwise every visit shows as Direct.

Your admin analytics has a Links tab that builds tagged links. A UTM tag is a small label added to the end of a link, such as the channel and campaign name, so analytics can tell which post sent a visit. Without it, a LinkedIn visit and a typed address look the same.

How to check: click one tagged link yourself and see it appear under the right channel in the dashboard. The enquiry form saves a fixed source and no UTM, so even a tagged visit cannot be tied to the enquiry itself. That gap is real and is covered in the lesson on referrals.

Planning what to publish and where

Four kinds of proof, in the order that costs least.

1 Fix the factsyear, profiles, name
2 Own-build storyHV Verse, honest
3 Question pagesfrom real enquiries
4 Client case studywith approval

The plan is small on purpose. A studio with a few visitors a day does not need a content calendar. It needs a handful of pages that answer the questions a buyer asks, plus a few places that confirm you exist. Here is the order, with where each kind of work lives.

CContent: answers and stories on your own siteWrite what buyers ask, not what sounds clever.

Collect the questions that arrive in every enquiry and in your calls: what a website costs, how long it takes, what a page that brings enquiries needs, what AI automation can do for a small firm. These are the topics your files suggest. Each page answers one question, ends with one next step, and uses the words a shop owner would use. The 10 FAQ answers and the Google Business Profile post texts already exist as drafts, so the first pages are partly written.

One honest build story beats five generic posts. Tell the HV Verse build as problem, approach, result, including what did not go well. A buyer reads honesty as skill.

PPublic presence: listings and mentionsSame facts, in places buyers already look.

Your files show a LinkedIn company page and a Justdial listing, while Crunchbase, Product Hunt, AlternativeTo and Wellfound are on a to-do list with unknown status. For a services firm the useful ones are local business listings and service directories where clients leave reviews. Product Hunt style sites suit app launches more than a studio, so treat them as low priority. List only what is true, and use the same name, year and link everywhere.

SSocial: showing the content to peopleLinkedIn first, with real screens.

The social plan is a draft with 3 posts a week on LinkedIn and Instagram, 2 on Facebook, 1 YouTube Short a week and light X use. That is more than a small team can keep up. The files suggest starting with LinkedIn only, posted from the founders' profiles plus the company page, using screenshots of your own builds so nobody has to appear on camera. Open decisions remain: who posts, and whether faces appear. Decide those before starting.

Most of the work is writing, and you already know how to publish pages on your own site. The tools matter at one point: when you share the page and want to learn whether anyone came from it. Here is the full routine, using your own admin analytics plus the free tools you already have.

1Write the case study from a fixed outlineProblem, approach, result, next step.

Use five short headings: who it was for, the problem, what you built, what changed, and what you would do differently. Put one screenshot of real work under each heading. Add the service name so the page links to the matching service page. End with "Discuss your project". Keep claims you can prove. For HV Verse you can say things such as which apps were built and when it launched. Do not invent client numbers; where no number is on file, write that plainly or leave it out.

2Make the tagged linkUse the Links tab, then post the tagged version.

Open the admin analytics, go to the Links tab, pick the page, pick the channel (for example LinkedIn post), keep or edit the campaign name, then copy the generated link. The tool adds source, medium and campaign labels for you. Post only that link. Menu names in admin or any tool can change, so look for the link maker if the tab is called something else.

3Share it where buyers areOne post on LinkedIn, one line in your profile.

On LinkedIn, post from a founder profile and the company page. Say in two sentences what the project was and what you learned, then the tagged link. Add the link to your profile's featured or website field, again tagged with a bio channel. Avoid pasting the same text on every platform; one real post is worth more.

4Check the source in analyticsOnce a week, read the Campaigns and channels tables.

In the developers tab of the admin analytics, look at where sessions come from and the Campaigns table (which groups visits by campaign name). Switch the Attribution control between Last touch and First touch to see both the channel that brought the visit and the channel that first introduced the visitor. Also look at Top pages to confirm that the case study is being read.

A free extra: Google Search Console, if it is set up for developers.haritt.com. Open it, choose Performance, and look at the queries and pages lists. Your files claim it is set up but hold no data, so confirm it shows real numbers first. Page and query rows show whether the new pages begin to appear for the questions you wrote them for. Menu names may change, so use the search box inside the tool if you cannot find a screen.

Made-up example for one month. Bar widths are not to scale.

1
Your last real picture (30 days to 13 Oct, real visitors only) was 301 sessions and 633 page views, with about 70 percent of sessions engaged. That is a small base. With numbers this small, one extra enquiry changes a percentage a lot, so read counts first and percentages second. A month of data from one proof push will not prove anything on its own, but it will show direction.
2
Read the numbers in the order a buyer moves. First, did people arrive from the tagged link (Campaigns table, channel table)? Second, did they read the page (Top pages, and engaged time)? Third, did they move towards you (view_contact, form_start, enquiry_sent events, all counted by your own counter)? Fourth, and most important, were the enquiries the right kind? Open the admin inbox and read each one. An enquiry with a budget band chosen, a real brief and a sensible timeline is worth more than five thin ones.

Made-up example, only to show the method: say a case study gets 60 visits in a month, 10 people start the form and 3 send it, of which 2 fit your services. Do not celebrate 3 enquiries or worry about 5 percent. Ask whether the 2 good ones are more than you had before the page, and whether any of them mention the case study when you ask how they found you. When it is not working

Content work fails quietly. Nothing breaks, so it is easy to give up or to blame the wrong thing. Work through the causes below from cheapest to check to hardest, and stop at the first clear culprit. The diagnosis box above lists the usual patterns, so tap the one closest to what you see.

1Is the tracking telling the truth?Click your own tagged link before blaming anything.

If a post got real reactions but the dashboard shows zero visits, the link is the suspect. Click your tagged link in a private window and see whether a visit appears under the right channel. Remember too that analytics blockers hide some visitors, so small gaps are normal. A large gap means a broken or untagged link.

2Is it the page?Look at engaged time and where people leave.

Open the page on a phone as a stranger would. Is the first screen clear about who it is for and what it solved? Walls of text, huge images that load slowly and a buried button all show up as short engaged time and no form starts. Fix the page before anything else, because a better post cannot rescue a page nobody reads.

3Is it the topic or the audience?Read who is replying and who is enquiring.

If posts get reactions from other developers while buyers stay silent, you are writing for peers. Buyers want a problem solved in their words. Compare the questions in real enquiries with your topics. Change the topic to one a buyer actually asked.

4Is it missing proof or reach?Count the evidence and the places.

If the page is readable, buyers are arriving and still not enquiring, proof is thin: add the quote, the approved logo or a clearer before and after. If very few people arrive at all, you are not in enough places: one post a week from three profiles can beat three posts from one.

To retest, change one thing, keep a note of what you believed, and give it a fair run of a few weeks before judging. A small log with date, belief, change, result and decision is enough. Because your traffic is small, resist deciding after three days.

Keeping the proof growing

Make proof a habit that follows each project, then revisit the plan.

Deliver the projectdo good work
Ask for approvalname, quote, screenshot
Write it upproblem, approach, result
Share and tagLinkedIn, profiles, site
New enquiriesask how they found you
1
Once the first pieces exist, the work becomes a loop. A delivered project becomes a case study with the client's approval, the case study brings visibility on search and LinkedIn, visibility brings enquiries, and enquiries become the next project. Your files already point to this as the loop to start with, and HV Verse lets you begin before any client agrees.
2
Make the ask part of every project, not an afterthought. At the start, say that you may write a short case study if they agree. At the end, ask for a two-line quote and permission to name them. Keep the answer in the enquiry notes so the approval is on record. If a client says no, respect it and write a no-name version only if they allow it.
3
Revisit this plan after you have a month of tagged data and a handful of enquiries read in full. By then you should know which topics bring buyers, whether the form fits the proof, and whether referrals (next lesson) or search ads (later) are the better next step. Keep the same facts everywhere, and update the old case studies when the services change.
