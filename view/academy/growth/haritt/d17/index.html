Why rules and honesty are part of growth

For a small studio sold on trust, one false claim or one unwanted message costs more than it earns.

ShortcutClaim it, send it

Fast, until someone checks.

vs
Safe growthProve it, ask first

Slower, and it keeps trust.

1
Ethics in marketing sounds like a lecture, but for Haritt Developers it is a practical matter. You sell to small business owners who are deciding whether to trust a stranger with their website. They will compare your site, your Justdial listing and your Google profile. If the three disagree, or a message arrives that they did not ask for, you lose the one thing a studio sells before it has any clients: trust. Rules about consent, privacy and honest claims also protect you from penalties and from losing an account on a platform you rely on.
2
Here is what you already do well, from your files. The form has a consent tick and a privacy notice. There are no ads. Case studies are promised only with the client's approval. You also have your own rule: no email goes out without your go. Those are good habits and this lesson builds on them.
3
There are also real risks on file. The founding year is claimed three ways: About says founded in 2026, your own story is 2020 and relaunched in 2026, and Justdial says established 2018. The Google Business Profile expects a real service-area setup with honest details, and its live status is unknown. A Justdial claim needs real ownership of the listing. And cold email or WhatsApp outreach needs consent or a clear legal basis.
Do you need this now? Yes, as a short check before each send, post or listing change. It takes minutes, and it is free. The biggest job right now is to make the year and other public claims the same everywhere before you build more visibility, because more visibility means more people will notice the conflict.

Go through the items one by one. For each, the question is the same: can you show proof, or have you permission? If not, change the claim or ask first.

APublic claimsYears, team size, results, clients.

A public claim is any statement about your studio that a visitor could check: when you started, how many people work there, what you have built, who you worked for. How to check: list every claim on your site, social profiles and listings, and write next to each what proves it. Anything with no proof should be removed or reworded. The founding-year conflict is the first item to settle. Choose the line you can stand behind and use it everywhere.

BPermission to messageConsent or a clear legal basis.

India has rules for commercial messages and a data-protection law. The names to look up are TRAI, DLT and DPDP. Do not rely on a summary, including this one. Read the current rules on the official sources, or ask a qualified adviser, before you send to people who have not asked to hear from you. How to check: for each person on an outreach list, can you say why they would expect your message?

CPrivacy and trackingWhat you collect, and what you say you collect.

Your analytics counter is first-party and collects counts, not names. The GA4 property (a Google analytics tool) was added on 20 October, and whether it is receiving the enquiry event cannot be checked from files. Your privacy text and your tracking must say the same thing. The privacy text needs aligning on your own list. How to check: read the privacy page and compare each sentence with what the site actually does.

DAccount rulesPlatforms can suspend honest mistakes.

A blocked Google account check is not something to work around. Fix the real problem and follow the steps the platform gives. A service-area business with no public office must set that up the way Google asks, rather than showing an address it does not have.

A claims register is a plain table. It is the quickest way to find mismatches and to stop new ones. Make a sheet with the five columns shown above, then fill it in one pass through your site, your social profiles and your listings. Menu names change, so if the dropdown menu is not where you expect, search the Data menu for validation.

WWhat goes in firstThe three things people compare.

Start with the founding year, the place and the service area, and your list of services. Then add team and results claims. If you are not sure about a number, such as how many projects you have done, leave it out. A smaller, true statement is better than a larger one you cannot prove.

TTestimonials and resultsOnly real, only approved.

Never write a testimonial that did not come from a client, and never show a logo without permission. When a client approves a quote, save the approval message with the claim. For results, state what happened in plain terms, or say nothing. The site already says case studies appear only with client approval. Keep that promise exactly.

PPersonal namesCheck your own rule.

Your own rule is that personal names are not shown on public pages, with a few exceptions you chose. Before you publish a case study, a post or a profile, check that names, faces and screenshots follow the same rule, for you and for the client.

How to read the register. The Fix count is your to do list, and it should fall each month. Rows with Proof saying none on file are the risk, because a visitor can check them. The same claim with different wording in two rows is a mismatch. Sort by Where it appears (Data, then Create a filter) to compare the three places a buyer looks: your site, Justdial and the Google profile. Review the Checked on column every few months, since a claim can be true once and stale later.

These three questions cover most of what matters day to day. They are not legal advice. For a firm answer on the rules that apply to you, use the official sources or a professional.

OOutreachSmall, personal and honest.

Send one-to-one messages to people with a clear reason. Say who you are, why you are writing and what you want. Never hide the sender or invent a reason. Your intro emails currently have no unsubscribe link, and the footer says to reply STOP. Whoever replies STOP goes on your Do not email list straight away, and you never contact them again. Keep your sending small until you add a proper way out and check the rules.

TThe enquiry form and your dataKeep what you need.

The form collects name, email, company, website and project details, and it records a consent tick. Those details are personal data, and they sit in your database and your inbox. Limit who can open the inbox, as it already requires an access key. Do not paste enquiries into shared documents or tools you have not checked. If someone asks you to delete their details, do so and note it.

TTracking and consentSay what you do.

Your own counter does not use cookies or user IDs, and it respects Do Not Track. GA4 is a separate Google tool, and it may set its own cookies. The group site has no cookie banner today, and consent is opt-out only. For the Developers site, check what GA4 actually does and that your privacy page describes it. Menu names in tools change, so check the current settings pages.

How to check readiness for outreach. You are ready to message people you do not know when four things are true: you have a clear reason for each person, a simple way for them to say stop, a list of those who have said no, and a reading of the current official rules that you have written down in your own words. If any of these is missing, stay with warm contacts and referrals. They need less paperwork and give better replies.

How to read the stop list. Count entries per month. A few is normal. A jump after one batch means the list or the wording was wrong, so pause, check how those people came to be on your list, and rebuild it from people who know your work. A bounce means a bad address, so remove it and check the source. Zero entries with many sends can also mean replies are not being read, so check your inbox for STOP replies too.

Ethics is working when nothing unpleasant happens, so you need a way to notice small signs. Keep a short pre-launch check at the top of your claims sheet and use it before every send, post or listing change. It can be five lines: Is every claim true and provable? Do I have approval for any name, quote or screenshot? Does the person have a reason to expect this message? Is there a way to say stop? Does it match the other places where we say the same thing?

When something goes wrong

Stop first, then find the cause.

What happened?

Tap what you see. The likely culprit shows here.

Most likelyA detail does not match

Most likelyUnchecked claim

Choose one version you can prove and fix every page.

Most likelyMissing approval

Remove it now, then ask in writing before reuse.

Most likelyOut of date text

Check each tool, and update the page.

Most likelyVerification or rules

Follow the official steps. Do not work around the check.

The order matters. First, stop the harm: pause the sending, take down the claim, remove the name. Second, say sorry to the person in plain words, without excuses. Third, find the cause by asking how it happened, not who did it. Was there no check? Was the list built from the wrong source? Was a claim copied from an old page?

1Was it a list problem?Where did the names come from?

Write down the source of every contact list. A list built from people who know your work is safe ground. A list bought, scraped or copied from a directory is not. If you cannot say how a person came to be on your list, take them off.

2Was it a claim problem?Was it true on the day?

Compare the wording with your register and the proof. A claim can be true once and stale later, such as a team size or a service you no longer offer. Fix the wording and the date you checked it.

3Was it a tool problem?Did a setting do something you did not expect?

Open the tool's settings and read what it records and sends. A new analytics tag, a form plugin or an email tool can collect more than you think. Compare it with your privacy page.

Fix and retest. Change the process, not only the instance. If the cause was a stale claim, add it to the register with a review date. If the cause was a list, write down where every list comes from. Then run the same pre-launch check on the next send and see if the signals stay quiet. If you are unsure about a legal point, ask a qualified adviser before continuing.

1
Start by choosing one founding-year line you can prove, and use it on the About page, the Justdial listing and the Google profile. Then build the claims register. After that, read the official pages on messaging and data rules once, and write down what applies to you in a few lines. Finally, put the five-line check at the top of your sheet.
2
Revisit this lesson before your first outreach batch, before you publish your first case study, and before you add any paid advertising. Each of those brings new rules. Ad platforms have their own policies, and a campaign is a new place to make a claim. Your plan says no paid ads for the first 90 days, which gives you time to get the foundations in order.
3
Keep one more thing in mind. Rules and platform settings change, and so do the names of menus in the tools you use. Treat this lesson as a method, not a final answer. Each time you plan something new, ask the same plain questions, check the current official pages, and write down what you decided and the date you decided it. A small dated note is often all you need to show, later, that you acted in good faith.
