Part 1 of 6

What your numbers are for, and which few to watch

A dashboard answers three questions: are we on track, what changed, what do we do next.

Goalmore qualified enquiries
KPIenquiry conversion rate
Inputssessions, contact views, starts
Outcomequalified, calls, proposals
1
A shop owner does not read forty charts. They look at a few numbers on one screen: how many came in, how many bought, how many left unhappy. A KPI (key performance indicator) is one such number, tied to a goal. For Haritt Developers the goal is to win project enquiries from businesses, and the number that shows progress is the share of visitors who send an enquiry. A dashboard puts a handful of these together so you can answer three questions in a minute: are we on track, what changed since last time, and what should we do next.
2
There is a trap to avoid, called a vanity metric. It is a number that looks nice but changes no decision, like total page views or follower counts on their own. From your files, 633 page views in 30 days sounds like something, but it does not tell you whether to write a new page or fix the form. Prefer actionable metrics, where you can see cause and effect, such as the share of contact page visitors who start the form. Also split numbers into leading ones that move first, like form starts, and lagging ones that move later, like proposals sent.
Do you need this now? A simple weekly sheet, yes, from this week. A Looker Studio dashboard, later. GA4 was added on 20 Oct, so it holds almost no history. Building polished charts on a few days of data would just show you noise. Start the sheet now, which costs nothing, and build the Looker Studio report once GA4 has about four weeks of clean data and you have checked the tracking from the previous lesson.

The real numbers on file are only from the 13 Oct screenshot: 211 visitor-days (daily devices added up, not people), 301 sessions, 633 page views, 70.1 percent engaged sessions and 38 seconds average engaged time. The enquiry rate and form completion are not in any file, and "Returning 0" in the same screenshot looks like a counting bug. So begin by writing real values down.

Reports in GA4 are full of words that sound alike, so start with what each one means, where it sits and what to do. Two screens above carry most of them: Reports, then Acquisition, then Traffic acquisition, and Reports, then Engagement, then Landing page. Menu names change, so look for the nearest match. The example numbers are made up. Your real figures from the admin counter on 13 Oct (301 sessions, 633 page views, 70.1 percent engaged, 38 seconds) came from your own counter, not GA4, so expect GA4 to differ. Judge each number against your own last four weeks.

1Active users, new users and sessionsWho and how often.

A user is a device or browser, so one person on a phone and a laptop counts twice. A session is one visit, ending after 30 minutes of inactivity. New users are first visits. Sessions are the better number for you, about 10 a day at the 13 Oct reading. A rise means search, listings or posts reach more people, not that they are the right people. A fall with no change on your side points to a ranking or listing change. Action: look at channels for the date it started.

2Engagement rate and engagement timeDid they stay and look?

An engaged session lasts 10 seconds or more, has a key event, or has two or more page views. Engagement rate is engaged sessions divided by all sessions. It sits as a column in Traffic acquisition. Average engagement time per session is how long the page was in focus. A sudden drop in one channel means that source sends the wrong people or lands them on a broken page. Action: open the landing page report for that channel and test on a phone. Do not chase a higher rate for its own sake.

3Key events and session key event rateThe number that pays the bills.

Once enquiry_sent is marked as a key event, Key events counts how many times it fired, and Session key event rate is the share of sessions with one. Read the two together by channel. Many sessions and zero key events is a message or page problem. Few sessions and a high rate is a channel to feed. With a handful of enquiries, one extra changes the percentage a lot, so use longer date ranges.

4Channel and source or mediumWhere visits came from.

Each session is put in a default channel such as Organic Search, Direct, Referral, Organic Social or Email. Source and medium is the finer view, like linkedin / social. It depends on your UTM tags. If most visits show as Direct, untagged links are hiding the truth. Action: tag the links you post, then compare again in a week. Do not read a tiny channel's rate as a trend.

5Landing pagesWhich page they entered on.

The Landing page report shows the first page of each visit, with sessions, new users, engagement time and key events. A page with many sessions and no key events needs a clearer next step or proof near the form. A page with few sessions but several key events deserves more links to it. Action: add the contact link or a build story to the weak page, change only that, and compare in four weeks.

6EventsWhich actions happened.

Reports, then Engagement, then Events lists every event with its count. Find enquiry_sent here and compare it with your inbox. Compare cta_contact, form_start and enquiry_sent to see where people give up. A gap of more than one or two between GA4 and the inbox means a missing event or a double count. Action: repeat the DebugView test from the previous lesson.

Data Studio, earlier called Looker Studio, is Google's free report builder. It reads from GA4, Search Console and Google Sheets, and it refreshes by itself, so the report is always current when you open it. Do this after a few weeks of clean GA4 data. A good first report has one page and no more than eight items.

1Create the report and connect GA4Start from a blank report.

Go to the Looker Studio site and sign in with the same Google account that can see GA4. Click Create, then Report. When asked for data, choose the Google Analytics connector, pick the account and the Haritt Developers property and press Add. Rename the report at the top.

2Add the top row of scorecardsFive numbers.

Use Add a chart, then Scorecard. Make one each for sessions, engagement rate, key events (set to enquiry_sent only), the enquiry conversion rate and, from a Google Sheet, qualified leads. Set the date range to Last 28 days and turn on comparison with the previous period, so each card shows the change.

3Add a trend and a channel tableWhat changed and where from.

Add a time series chart of sessions and enquiry_sent by week. Add a table with the dimension Session default channel group, and the metrics sessions, engagement rate and key events. Add a second table with landing page and the same metrics, so you can see which page brings enquiries.

4Add Search Console and the lead sheetSearch terms and real outcomes.

Add data again, choose Search Console, and select the site developers.haritt.com once it is verified and linked. Add a table of queries and clicks. Then add data from a Google Sheet that holds your weekly lead log (qualified, calls, proposals). Without it, the dashboard ends at the enquiry, which is where GA4 ends.

5Label, share and checkSo someone else can read it.

Add a text box under each chart saying what it answers. Add one note box for launches, such as "Start a project form changed on this date". Use Share to give view access to your co-founder. Open it on your phone, since you will read it there.

Keep the layout plain: numbers on top, trends in the middle, tables below. If a chart does not change any decision, delete it. Never build more than one page until the first page is used every week.

Part 4 of 6

The weekly update routine

Twenty minutes, same day each week, same order, written down.

Check tagsFill the sheetRead the dashboardWrite 3 linesPick one action

A dashboard no one reads is decoration. The weekly routine is what makes it useful. Pick one day, such as Monday morning, and follow the same order, because comparing like with like is the whole point.

1Check that tracking is aliveTwo minutes.

Open Realtime, check the tag, and compare the week's enquiry_sent count with the inbox count. If they differ by more than one or two, fix that before you read anything else.

2Fill the weekly sheetFive numbers, written by hand.

Record sessions, enquiries, qualified enquiries, calls held and proposals sent for the week. The first two come from GA4 or the dashboard. The last three come from your inbox notes, because no event records them. Define each one in writing: for example, a qualified enquiry is one that fits your services and has a real budget and timeline. Add targets only after four weeks of data, otherwise you will be guessing.

3Read the dashboard in a fixed orderTotals, trend, channels, pages.

Start with sessions and enquiries, then the trend line, then channels, then landing pages. At roughly 10 sessions a day, one week is very noisy: three enquiries one week and none the next may mean nothing. Compare rolling four weeks to the four before, and treat anything less than a clear trend as a question, not a conclusion.

4Write three linesWhat changed, why you think so, what next.

For example: "Sessions up, enquiries the same. The new post brought visitors who left the contact page. Next: add a build story above the form." Keep these lines in the sheet. They are the memory of the business.

5Pick one actionOnly one.

Choose one change for the coming week and note the date it ships. If you change five things, the next report cannot tell you which one mattered.

1
A single overall rate hides where the problem is. A funnel counts people at each step and shows the share lost between steps. Yours is visible in the dashboard from Visitors to Services or Work, to Contact, to Started form, to Sent enquiry. Add a fourth step by hand from your sheet: qualified leads. In GA4 you can also build a funnel in Explore, then Funnel exploration, listing the events as steps. At your traffic this is more for learning than for exact rates.
2
A segment is a slice of users, such as by channel, device, region or service interest. These slices are already possible in your dashboard. Compare the enquiry rate for mobile with desktop, and for Organic Search with Direct. Be careful with tiny groups: three visits from a channel with one enquiry is a 33 percent rate that means almost nothing. Cohort tables, which follow groups of people over time, add little for a lead site of this size, so skip them for now. Also note that "Returning 0" looks like a bug, so do not use returning-visitor numbers until it is checked.
3
Attribution decides which channel gets the credit for an enquiry. Someone might find you on Justdial, later see a LinkedIn post, then search your name and write. GA4 will credit the last click, probably Organic Search for the brand name, which is only the final step. Your brand name already ranked #1 and #2 on 14 Oct, so branded search will always look good. Use three views together: GA4 channels, the tagged link in the enquiry once the form captures it, and what the person tells you in the "How did you hear about us?" answer. When they disagree, trust the person's own answer for small numbers.
4
To read the funnel screen: look at the share that survives from one step to the next, not only the end. A big loss between the first two steps points to the page or message. A big loss between starting and sending the form points to form friction. At your size this is for learning, since one enquiry moves the share a lot.

When a number moves the wrong way: investigate before you act

Match the pattern to the likely cause, change one thing, and check four weeks later.

What do your numbers show?

Tap what you see. The likely culprit shows here.
Most likelyLess traffic

Check channels and Search Console. Did a post, listing or ranking change?

Most likelyPage, form or tracking

Test the form yourself, check enquiry_sent against the inbox, then the contact page funnel.

Most likelyWrong audience

Look at the channel and landing page. Do these visitors reach the contact page?

Most likelyBroken page or bad source

Check pages and channels for the day it dropped. Test on mobile.

Most likelyOffer or message mismatch

Read the project text and budget band. Tighten the headline.

Most likelyTracking broke

Check the tag in Realtime first, before assuming a real collapse.

1
When a number moves the wrong way, resist changing something straight away. Work through it like a doctor reading symptoms: first confirm the measurement is true, then find where in the funnel it changed, then find who is affected, and only then guess the cause. Tap the patterns above for the usual meanings.
2
A useful order is this. First, check tracking, since a broken tag looks exactly like a collapse. Second, look at the date the change began and write down everything you changed or posted around that date. Third, split by channel, by device and by landing page, to see whether the drop is everywhere or in one place. Fourth, look at the quality side: read the actual enquiries. If the numbers are small, reading ten enquiries tells you more than any chart.
3
Then change one thing and note it on the dashboard. After four weeks compare against the weeks before. If the fix worked, keep it and write down why. If not, undo it if it hurt, and try the next likely cause. If the problem is only a bad week with a handful of visits, do nothing and watch another week. Revisit this lesson's setup when you add a new channel, start paid ads or change the form, because each adds new things to measure and new ways for the numbers to mislead you.
