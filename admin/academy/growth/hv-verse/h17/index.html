Time is the budget, so count it.

1
Three ideas sit under this lesson. CAC (customer acquisition cost) is what it costs to win one user: the money and effort spent on marketing divided by the new users it brought. Funnel maths multiplies the conversion rates step by step to forecast how many visitors you need for a goal. Budget allocation splits a limited budget between what already works, what looks promising and small experiments. A small gym owner uses all three when deciding how to spend 10,000 rupees in a month.
2
For HV Verse the usual versions do not fit directly. There is no revenue, so the standard pair of LTV (what a customer is worth over their stay) and CAC cannot be worked out. The cash cost per user is Rs 0, and the revenue per user is Rs 0. Your files say it plainly: your budget is zero, by decision, and "time is the budget". Your social plan includes about 30 minutes a day of outreach. So the honest version of this maths counts hours and activated users, not rupees and sales.
3
This still matters, because hours are not free. Thirty minutes a day is about 15 hours in a month. If those hours went into groups, posts and fixes that brought no activated students, you paid a real price with your time. The maths below tells you where the next hour works hardest.
Do you need this now? Yes for the time version, which is free and starts this week. No for the money version: do not calculate ROAS, break-even or payback until you have spend and revenue. The paid ads lesson explains when spend might start (after three months of real numbers, with your approval).

The one ratio to start with is cost per activated user: the effort you spent divided by the users who reached the activation step. Your own files recommend it instead of CAC for exactly this stage. Here is how to build it in a free Google Sheet. Menu names can change, so look for the closest label.

1Create the sheetKeep it to one tab.

Go to Google Sheets, start a blank spreadsheet, and name it for the growth log. Put the five headings from the picture in row 1. Use one row per week and per channel, so the sheet stays easy to scan.

2Fill in time and resultsKeep the same names every time.

Each day, add the minutes you spent in each channel. Each week, take the activated users by tagged source from the dashboard and enter them in column D. If a source cannot be separated yet, write "not measured" instead of guessing.

3Add the ratioDivision by zero gives an error.

In column E, type a formula that divides minutes by activated users, wrapped so that it stays empty when there are none. In Sheets the form is: if the activated cell is above 0, divide, otherwise show nothing. Drag it down the column.

1
A made up example, to show the arithmetic: say in one week you spend 150 minutes in college WhatsApp groups and 5 users reach first task done, which is 30 minutes per activated user. Instagram takes 120 minutes and brings 1, which is 120 minutes per user. Those numbers are invented, but the lesson is clear: the channel with the lowest minutes per activated user deserves your next hour.
2
How do you read the sheet after a month? Look at each channel over several weeks, not one. A single week with one activated student is a story, not a rate. A channel is working when its minutes per activated user stays low or falls while the number of activated users holds or grows. It is not working when minutes keep rising with no matching rise in users, or when the activated users it brings do not come back after seven days. For that reason, add one more column later: how many of the activated users from that channel were still active a week afterwards. A cheap activation that vanishes is not a good result.
HHow to read the sheetWhat each column means and what to do about a bad pattern.

Minutes per activated user. It is minutes spent divided by students who reached the first win. A lower number is better, but only compare it with your own earlier weeks and your other channels. Do not look for an outside benchmark. A single week is noise, so read the Summary tab, which adds up a month. If this number is high for every channel, the problem sits inside the app (the first screen or first task), not in the channel. If only one channel is high, move some hours away from it. If it keeps rising week after week, stop that channel and write why.

Activated users per channel. This is the size of the result. A channel with few minutes and 1 user looks great and means nothing. Wait until a channel has at least a few activated users before moving hours. Also read the not measured rows: if most of your users have no source, your links are untagged, and every ratio above is a guess. Use the Links tab in the admin to tag each link first.

Still active at day 7. This column checks quality. Compare it with the activated count in the same row. If many activate but few stay, the channel brings curious visitors and the product does not hold them yet. The action is to read the retention lesson, not to send more people.

1
A funnel forecast takes a goal such as "get 20 students to finish a first task in HV Reset" and turns it into a list of what must happen. You divide step by step, using your own measured rates, and label every guess as an assumption. Run it backwards: if 40% of signed-in students finish a first task, then 20 activated needs 50 signed-in students. If 25% of visitors sign in, you need 200 visitors. These percentages are made up, only to show how you do it. Your real rates are not on file, because the funnel counts are not reliable yet.
2
Three habits make the forecast useful. Change one rate at a time, so you see which stage matters most. Allow for time, since a student may sign in days after the first visit. And compare your real results with the forecast every week, then update it. The weakest rate decides the plan. If sign-in is the leak, share more of what students want. If activation is the leak, work on the first screen and the first task, before sending more people.
3
You can also keep a forecast with three scenarios in the same sheet: a conservative, a base and an optimistic set of rates. Use the conservative one when you decide how many hours to commit.
4
Here is how to tell that the forecast helped. After four weeks, put your forecast beside the real figures for each step. If real sign-ins are far below the forecast, the top of the funnel was too optimistic, and you should lower the rate you assumed or work on the message that brings people in. If sign-ins match but activation is far below, the issue sits inside the app. Treat each gap as a question to answer, not as a failure. Over time the guessed rates are replaced by measured ones, and the forecast becomes something you can trust when you plan your hours.
5
How to read the forecast. Compare the Visits needed with the real visits you can reach in your hours. If the gap is huge, change the goal, not the maths. After four weeks, put real beside forecast for each step. Real sign ins far below the forecast mean the top of the funnel was too optimistic, so improve the message. Sign ins on target but activation far below mean the leak is inside the app. Keep a conservative column and use it when you decide your hours.

Split your hours, and set a stop rule

A small portfolio of effort: protect what works, back what shows promise, cap experiments.

CoreWhat already works

Protect it and improve it.

PromisingEarly good signs

Scale step by step.

ExperimentsSmall and capped

A written guess and a stop rule.

Budget allocation means choosing where the next unit of effort goes. A common rule of thumb is to put most of it into what works, some into what looks promising and a small share into capped experiments. It is only a rule of thumb, but it prevents two mistakes: spreading hours evenly over everything, and pouring everything into one channel past the point where each extra hour brings less.

With 30 minutes a day, a practical split might be most of the time in the one channel with the lowest minutes per activated user, a smaller part in the second, and a few sessions a month for something new. Before each experiment, write a stop rule: for example, "if after 10 hours this brings no activated users, stop and review". The numbers in that sentence are yours to choose.

Judge channels on activated users you would not have had anyway, not only on who gets the credit in a report. A friend who would have found the app anyway still shows as a WhatsApp visit. Keep this in mind when two channels look similar.

Review the split once a month, not every day. A new channel needs a fair run before you judge it, and a channel that worked last month may fade as the same college groups see your posts repeatedly. If you notice that more effort in one place brings each extra hour fewer users than the one before, that is the point to move some time elsewhere. Write the decision and the reason in the log so that you can explain it later.

WWhen money entersAny paid spend needs your approval first.

If you ever add a paid test, the ratios change. Cost per activated user becomes spend divided by activated users, and you should read it together with day 30 return. Break-even ROAS, which is 1 divided by gross margin, is only meaningful once you earn revenue. Today it is not applicable, so leave it out of your sheet.

1
When the maths gives a strange answer, the problem is more often in the data than in the strategy. Start with the counting: are returning users showing zero, are visits missing a source, did you change a link halfway through the week? Then look at the funnel for the weakest rate, and only then judge the channel.
2
Change one thing and wait. If you move your hours from Instagram to WhatsApp groups and also change the post and the link, you cannot say what caused the result. After a change, give it a few weeks, record the new minutes per activated user, and compare with the earlier rows in your sheet. Keep what improved and drop what did not.
When to come back: after the three month review. If by then you have real counts, a tagged source for each visit and a few months of the log, you can judge whether a small paid test is worth proposing, and the money versions of these ratios start to apply.

Until then, the weekly habit is small: log your minutes each day, copy the activated users by source into the sheet once a week, read minutes per activated user for each channel, and decide where the next week's hours go. That is the whole of cost maths for a free product at your stage.
