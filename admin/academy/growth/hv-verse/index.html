What growth marketing means for HV Verse

Free apps change what growth means: activation, return visits and sharing, not sales.

Brand marketingThe signboard

Makes people remember and trust the name.

vs
Growth marketingThe whole path

First hello, first use, coming back, telling a friend.

1
Think of a small bakery near a college. It has a signboard that people recognise and trust, and on some days it puts out a chalk sign that says "10% off before noon". The signboard is brand marketing: it makes people remember you and feel safe choosing you. The chalk sign is performance marketing: you pay a little to get one clear action today, and you can see exactly what it cost. Growth marketing is a wider habit. It looks at the whole path a person takes with you, from the first time they hear of you to the day they come back and tell a friend, and it keeps improving that path through small experiments.
2
For HV Verse, this split matters more than it sounds, because your products are free. HV Reset, HV Hunt, HV Assess and HV AI do not take money from anyone, and there is no revenue model yet. That means nobody "buys" anything, so there is no sale to count and nothing for a chalk-sign campaign to improve. Your own decision already reflects this: organic only, no ads for now, revisit after three months of real numbers. So growth for HV Verse does not mean getting sales. It means getting a student to try an app, find real value in it, come back, and tell someone else.
3
There is also a brand point worth being honest about. The name HV Verse is only two or three weeks old, since the rename happened on 19 October, so there is very little brand history to build on yet. For now, "brand work" simply means keeping the name and the @thehvverse accounts consistent everywhere. The real work, and the part you can measure, is getting people through their first useful moment in an app.
Do you need this now? Yes. It is the foundation for every other lesson. If you do not know what stage your growth is stuck at, every later activity (posts, ads, emails) is a guess. This lesson is free and takes a few hours, and it prevents months of wasted effort.

A funnel is just the list of steps a person takes, with some people dropping off at every step. Imagine a tuition class: many parents hear about it, fewer visit, fewer enrol, and only some stay for the next year. The shape narrows like a funnel, and the useful question is always the same: which step loses the most people? Fix that one first, not the biggest or the most exciting one.
2
Your dashboard already has funnels, and you can read them as a story of one student. Notice what is missing, and why it matters. All of these funnels start at the app. Nothing records the step before: the Instagram post, the WhatsApp group message, the poster in a college. AARRR: five stages, one order of work

Activation first, then retention, then referral.

AcquisitionLater
ActivationFirst
RetentionSecond
ReferralThird

A funnel shows steps, but it does not tell you which part of the experience matters most for a free product. A well-known framework called AARRR, nicknamed "pirate metrics" because it sounds like a pirate, splits the journey into five stages. Acquisition: people arrive. Activation: they have a good first experience. Retention: they come back. Referral: they tell others. Revenue: they pay.

The reason this is useful is that it stops the usual mistake. When growth feels slow, people say "we need more traffic", but the real leak is often that visitors arrive and nothing good happens in the first few minutes. Buying more visitors in that situation just fills a leaking bucket faster. Looking at the stages side by side shows where the leak really is.

Our reading: for a free product, work in this order: Activation, then Retention, then Referral. Acquisition numbers exist, but the other three are not reliable or not measured today, and there is no point pushing more people in until the first experience is worth having. Revenue can wait until there is a revenue model.

Choosing your one number

A North Star metric and the few inputs that move it.

North StarWeekly users who finish a core action

A task done in HV Reset, or a job moved in HV Hunt.

InputsActivation, week-1 return, shares

The few numbers you can move each week.

Side numberVisitors

Rewards traffic, not value, so keep it secondary.

With four apps and many numbers, it is easy to end up watching everything and acting on nothing. A North Star metric solves that. It is the one number that best shows people getting real value from your product, and it comes before revenue, not after. A gym owner could watch revenue, footfall or Instagram likes, but the number that really shows members getting value might be "members who train three times a week". That is a North Star.

The reason it matters is that it points everything in the same direction. It also protects you from vanity metrics, such as total visits or sign-ups, which look good but say nothing about value. Your Ecosystem tab currently offers Visitors, Sessions, Engaged sessions and Activated, and its own note says these rows must not be added up as total people.

A good candidate for HV Verse

A good candidate for a free app is weekly active users who finish a core action: a task done in HV Reset, or a job moved in HV Hunt. Check it with four questions. Does it show real value to a student? Does it come before revenue? Can you move it every week? Can anyone understand it in a sentence? The weekly-active-users tile in the dashboard currently shows 0, so this number cannot be read yet. Fixing that count is the first job.

The few inputs that move it

Under the North Star, keep only a few smaller numbers that you can influence directly: the share of new users who reach activation, how many are still active after seven days, and how many students share an app link. Give each a weekly review. Keep visitors only as a side number, because it rewards traffic and not value.

Fit signals, false positives and the three kinds of lead.

9 of 20Likes it, not essential
4 of 20Could live without

Made-up example with 20 answers, only to show how to read the result.

Product-market fit sounds large, but for you it simply means: do enough students truly need what you made, and does your message make one group respond? A widely used rule of thumb is the Sean Ellis test, which is the question you just added. If at least 40 out of 100 users say "very disappointed", you have probably reached fit. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, and it needs a reasonable number of answers. With twenty answers, treat it as a hint.

In the made-up example above, 7 of 20 is 35 percent. That is close, so you would not celebrate or panic. You would read the open answers, check which group gave the "very disappointed" replies, and ask whether that group is the one you were going to aim at. Often the answer is surprising: the people who care most are not the group you chose.

TThree signals to read togetherOne number alone can mislead.

Survey: the fit question above. Behaviour: do people come back without a reminder? Your dashboard has D1, D7, D14 and D30 retention by first-visit day, plus weekly and monthly active devices, but the 13 October screenshot showed zeros for returning visitors, which looked like a counting bug. Fix that before trusting retention. Pull: do people arrive through friends or search without you pushing? Look at the Channels view for visits marked as shared by users.

WWhat counts as a lead for youA lead is a person with a reason to come back.

With no sale, the word changes. Write three kinds of lead on one page. The user lead is someone who passed activation: first task done in HV Reset, first job added in HV Hunt, test completed in HV Assess. The applicant lead is a Student Beta application, tracked by status. The institute lead is an organisation that might use the apps with students. Today there is no way to capture institute leads, so use a simple sheet or a short form until volume justifies more.

FFalse positives to avoidPraise that looks like fit but is not.

Friends and family saying "nice" is not fit. A burst of sign-ups from one WhatsApp forward is not fit. High visits with no repeat use is not fit. A single enthusiastic user who never returns is not fit. Count only people who did the core action.

Fit is rarely a clean yes or no. When the signals are weak, you do not give up. You find out whether the problem is the group, the message, the product or the measurement, because each has a different fix.

1Group problemRight product, wrong people.

Split the survey and applications by profession. If fresher job seekers score well on HV Hunt but school students do not, you have found your first group. Fix: aim the promise at the group that scores, and move the others to a waiting list.

2Message problemPeople do not understand what you offer.

Run the five-second test on three students. If they describe the product differently from how you do, the words are wrong. Fix: use their words. Retest by showing the new line to three new students.

3Product problemPeople understand it but do not get value.

CLabels on your links, so you can see where people came fromUTM tags tell you which post or ad sent each sign-up.

Right now, if ten students join the beta this week, you do not know whether they came from your Instagram bio, from a Reel, from a friend's WhatsApp message or from a Google search. They all arrive at the same form and look identical. A label fixes this. A label is a small piece of text added to the end of a web address, like ?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=beta-oct. The page looks exactly the same to the student, but your analytics can read the label and record that this visit came from Instagram, through a paid ad, as part of a campaign you named "beta-oct". These labels are called UTM tags.

Why this matters so much for ads: the ad platform will happily tell you how many clicks you got, but it only knows its own side. It cannot tell you how many of those people went on to become good users inside your product. Your own analytics can, but only if the visit arrived with a label. Without labels, the clicks you paid for and the users you gained become two separate stories that you cannot connect. With labels, you can say "the ₹2,000 I spent brought these 6 sign-ups, and 3 of them became good users."

What to do: use the UTM calculator on the Growth page to build one link for each place you share: bio, each post, each story, and later each ad. Keep the naming pattern the same every time, because "Instagram", "instagram" and "insta" are counted as three different sources.

HV Verse today: your links do not carry labels. That means even your free posts cannot be compared, so fix this first, and it costs nothing.

DA landing page that works on a phoneEvery slow second or extra field loses a click you already paid for.

The landing page is where a student arrives after clicking your ad, which in your case is the beta form. Nearly all your students will see it on a phone. Every second it takes to load, every extra field, every small button, every confusing line loses some of them. The painful part is that you have already paid for their click. A page that loses half of its visitors makes every ad twice as expensive, without you ever noticing.

How to check: open the form on your own phone using mobile data, not Wi-Fi. Time how long it takes to appear. Fill it in as a stranger would. Count the fields and ask whether you truly need each one. Then run the page through PageSpeed Insights, a free Google tool, and read what it says about mobile.

HV Verse today: unknown, so do this check now.

EEnough organic signal to learn fromCopy what already works for free. Wait for about 100 clicks of data.

Before paying, you should have seen some organic results: which posts students respond to, what words they use for their problem, which promise gets attention. This is valuable because your first ad should copy what already works for free. If you start ads with a guess, you are paying to learn what you could have learned from a free post.

There is also a numbers reason. A test with very few clicks proves nothing. Five sign-ups can easily become ten or two by pure luck, so you cannot tell if the ad was good. As a rough rule, you want at least about a hundred clicks before you start drawing conclusions, and more is better. That is part of why your decision to wait three months of organic data was a sensible one.

FMoney you can truly afford to lose, and a rule for when to stopDecide the limit while calm, and write it down.

The first campaign is a test. Think of the money as the price of an answer, not an investment you expect to get back. Decide the maximum amount, for example ₹2,000, and decide it while you are calm, not in the middle of a campaign. Many people raise the budget when results are poor because they feel they have already spent too much to quit. A written limit stops you from doing that.

Ad platforms offer a small tracking snippet, called a pixel, that you place on your site so the platform can see who visited and show them ads again. It is powerful, but it collects personal data, so your privacy text and consent settings must match before you install one. For a first test you do not need it, since labelled links are enough to measure. Keep the pixel for later and do it properly.

1Reach, impressions and frequencyWho saw it and how often

Reach is the number of different people who saw your ad. Impressions are the times it was shown, so the same person can add several. Frequency is impressions divided by reach, the average times each person saw the ad. In the table it sits in the second row of columns.

Good pattern: reach grows each day and frequency stays low in a short test. Bad pattern: reach flat, frequency climbing and click rate falling. That means the same small group sees the ad again and again, which is ad fatigue or an audience that is too small. Action: widen the audience, change the first line or picture, or end the ad. Do not raise the budget.

2CPMCost of a thousand views

CPM is what you pay for each 1,000 impressions. It tells you how expensive it is to be seen by this audience, before anyone clicks.

If CPM jumps while click rate stays the same, you are paying more for the same attention, often because the audience is narrow or in demand. Action: widen the audience a little, or test a different placement. If CPM is steady but clicks are poor, the problem is not the price of reach but the ad.

3Link clicks, CTR and CPCDid anyone act, and what each visit cost

Link clicks count clicks on the link in your ad. CTR is the share of impressions that became a link click. CPC is spend divided by link clicks. Add the link versions of these columns, because plain clicks also count likes and profile taps.

A low CTR with the right people points to a weak hook: the first line or the picture. A good CTR but poor sign-ups points past the ad, to the page. High CPC with a good CTR usually means high CPM. Action: write three new first lines and run them as three ads in the same ad set, then compare.

Once you have a likely culprit, change only that thing. This is the most important discipline of the whole process. If you change the ad, the page and the audience together, and results improve, you will not know which change helped, so you cannot repeat it. If they get worse, you will not know which hurt.

Write your guess in one sentence before you change anything: "I believe the form loses students because it has six fields. If I cut it to three, the sign-up rate will go from about 5 percent to about 8 percent." Make the change, run it again with the same audience and the same budget for the same length of time, and compare against the earlier week. If the sign-up rate moves as predicted, you were right and you keep the change. If it does not, your guess was wrong, and that is also useful: cross that one off and move to the next likely cause on your list.

Keep a simple test log with these columns: date, what you believed, what you changed, the result and the decision. After a few rounds, this log becomes your own marketing knowledge about your students, which no course can give you.

When the cost per good user is within the limit you set, do not jump to a large budget. Increase slowly, for example by about a fifth every few days, and watch whether the cost per good user holds. Results often get worse as you widen the audience, because the first people reached were the easiest ones. If the cost rises beyond your limit, step back to the last level that worked.

Rhythms fade for dull reasons. The meeting becomes long, the numbers are hard to find, or nobody leaves with a decision. Fix those, not the people. Cut the review to the shortest version that still ends with one test, one owner and one date. Keep one source of truth for each number, so you never spend the slot arguing about which figure is right.
2
If a test shows no change, that is still information. Log it, and do not call it a failure. If a test shows a change, ask whether something else changed too, such as a post going viral in a group chat. Repeat before you scale, and stop the ones that do not move the number after a fair try.
3
If the audit itself feels pointless, check whether the evidence column is empty. An audit built on opinion repeats what you already believed. Go back to the screenshots and exports. And if two ratings seem to contradict each other, for example measurement Building while experimentation is Gap, remember that they depend on each other: you cannot run a fair test until the counts can be trusted, so the measurement fix comes first.
