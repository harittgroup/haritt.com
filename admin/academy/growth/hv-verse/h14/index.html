Part 1 of 6

Retention: do students come back?

For free apps, coming back is the proof the product works. First fix the counter, then read it.

Leaky bucketKeep pouring in new students

Most leave after one visit, so you refill forever.

vs
Held bucketSome students stay

The curve flattens above zero and growth builds on it.

1
Retention means people keep coming back after the first visit. Say 100 students try HV Reset in one week and 20 are still using it a month later. Your one-month retention is 20 per cent. The opposite is churn, the share who stopped. For paid products churn costs money. For HV Verse, which is free with no revenue model, churn costs something just as real: the effort you spent to get that student, and the proof that the apps help.
2
Retention is also the best early sign of a product people want. If the share of students still active drops and then flattens above zero, a group is staying for good, and that is worth more than any number of one-time visits. If it falls to zero, no amount of marketing will fix it, because you would be pouring water into a bucket with no bottom.
Do you need this now? Partly. Reading retention is needed now, but you cannot do it yet: your dashboard shows Returning 0 and weekly and monthly active users 0, which is a counting fault, not a result. So the first job is the fix. Lifecycle messages (automatic emails timed to what a student does) are a later step. You already send welcome and Beta mails, but the sender address, the missing unsubscribe link and the unconfirmed bulk Beta send should be settled before you send more. In the meantime, ask Beta testers directly whether they still open the apps.

The rest of the lesson covers readiness, how to read the numbers in your dashboard and in GA4, what brings students back, how to plan and measure messages, and what to do when the curve is bad.

Messages feel like a quick win, but sent too early they can damage the one thing you cannot rebuild: whether students open your mail at all. Check each item below.

AThe return count worksNo working count, no way to tell if messages help.

Test it. Visit the site on a fresh browser today and again tomorrow, then check that Returning and the weekly active users tile move. If they stay at 0, debug before reading anything. Your files suggest the code and the dashboard may be using different names for the weekly and monthly records, but the cause is not confirmed.

BA meaningful action counts as activeRetention only means something if 'active' means something.

Count a first task, a job added or a test completed, not just opening the page. A student who logs in and leaves is not retained. This links to the activation definition in the earlier lesson.

CAn easy way to unsubscribeMail providers punish senders without one.

Your footer says reply STOP and you keep a do-not-email list, but there is no unsubscribe link. Gmail and Yahoo expect one-click unsubscribe and low complaint rates, and they have checked sender records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) since 2024. Re-check the current rules when you set this up. For thehvverse.com those records are not set yet.

DThe right senderStudents should see HV Verse, not an old name.

App mails still go out from an address on the old group domain. A switch to support@thehvverse.com is waiting for the Gmail Send-as step. Finish it first, so students recognise the sender.

EConsent for promotional mailSending without consent is the quickest way to lose trust.

A receipt or password reset is transactional. Anything that promotes needs consent. Welcome mails after a sign-in are fine. A weekly digest to people who only visited is not.

If three or more of these are red, spend your time on them. It is better to send nothing than to send mail that goes to spam.

Your admin dashboard has a retention heat table on each product tab. Each row is a cohort, a group of devices that first visited on the same day, for the last 14 days. The columns show how many came back exactly 1, 7, 14 and 30 days later. Read it three ways: down a column to see whether newer groups are better than older ones, across a row to see where it flattens, and by product to see which app holds people best.

The tiles above it show weekly active users (WAU), monthly active users (MAU) and stickiness, which is the average number of daily users divided by monthly users. A higher stickiness means people use it on more days of the month. All of these are based on the device through local storage, so a new browser or cleared storage looks like a new person. That is why the Returning 0 reading matters: with 60 device-days that were not new, zero returning is impossible.

GGA4 RetentionUser level, with a cookie.

In GA4 go to Reports, then Retention. You get a chart of new and returning users, an engagement-by-day view, and a cohort table where each row is users who first came in a week and each column shows who returned. It needs several weeks of data, and young cells stay empty. GA4 also follows a person who logs in across devices only in limited cases, so for HV Verse treat it as another device-level view. Use it as a check on your own table: if both say D7 is low, believe it. If GA4 shows returns and your counter shows none, the counter is faulty.

RReading small cohortsKeep it honest with small groups.

With a handful of students per day, one person moves a cell by tens of points. Add a few days together into a weekly cohort before drawing a conclusion. Also write the cohort size next to every percentage you share.

What brings students back, and a message plan

Product reasons first, then four small messages: welcome, first help, stalled nudge, win-back.

WelcomeAfter first sign-in
First-value helpIf no first action yet
Stalled nudgeQuiet for about a week
Win-backGone for a month
1
Messages cannot fix a product people forget. The strongest reasons to return are inside the apps. HV Reset has streaks and focus numbers in its own in-app dashboard, a tester Beta pass and points. HV Hunt holds a job pipeline that gets stale unless you update it, which is a natural reason to come back. HV Assess gives a one-time result, so its return reason is different: a retest later, or the 30-day plan. Ask what each app gives a student to come back for, and strengthen that before sending anything.
2
Then plan messages. Lifecycle messaging means automatic messages timed to where a student is in their journey. Today these are live: a welcome on first sign-in for HV Reset and HV Hunt, a Beta pass, a tester guide about five minutes later, a Beta reminder for quiet testers (at most once a week), a left-the-Beta mail, a welcome back, and a Beta thank-you for applications. There is nothing for HV Assess, no win-back mail and no weekly digest.

Give each message one job and one action. A first-value mail for HV Hunt should only say add your first job and link there. Judge messages by clicks, not opens, since Apple's mail privacy feature made open rates unreliable. Your Gmail setup cannot show opens at all, but the email click counter already records link clicks per campaign. A paid mail service gives delivery data later for a small cost, which you can consider if volume grows.

Run it and tell whether it worked

Send small, track clicks and return rates by cohort, and compare with students who did not get the message.

Fix senderAdd unsubscribeWrite 1 messageLabel its linksTest on yourself
Clicks on linkFirst action afterReturn at D7Compare to no-message groupLog result

Start with a single message, not a system. The best first candidate is a mail to students who signed in to HV Reset or HV Hunt but have not reached the first win. Write it as a plain note from the team, one sentence on what they can do in two minutes, and one link.

1Label the linksLabel every link so you can see what it brought.

Use a UTM label such as source email, medium lifecycle and campaign stalled-nudge. The dashboard's Campaigns panel and GA4's Traffic acquisition report will then show visits from that mail.

2Test on yourselfSee the mail as a student sees it.

Send it to your own addresses on a phone and check the sender name, the subject and where the link lands.

3Send to a small groupStart with a few, not everyone.

Keep a few similar students out of the send. Later you can compare them with those who got it. That gives you a fair comparison even without a testing tool. The bulk send to everyone in the Beta stays unsent until you confirm it.

4Read the resultRead the same numbers in the same order.

Look first at clicks, then at how many of the clickers reached the first action, then at the D7 return of this group against the group left out. A good result is clicks followed by action, then a higher return. High clicks with no action means the mail promised more than the page gave. Say 20 got the mail, 6 clicked and 2 did the action (made-up numbers): that is real but tiny, so keep logging before you call it a win.

Keep the rhythm gentle. The Beta reminder already caps itself at once a week, and a student who unsubscribes or replies STOP must go on the do-not-email list immediately. Fewer, better-timed mails beat a flood.

When retention looks bad, avoid sending more mail as the first reaction. Find the cause with the cheapest checks first.

1Trust the measurementIs the bad number real?

Run the two-day test, compare with GA4's returning users, and check cohort sizes. A cell based on three students says nothing.

2Read the patternLook at the shape, not one cell.

A sharp fall that levels off above zero is healthy. A steady slide to zero means no one finds lasting value. A fall for only one channel points to that audience.

3Talk to leaversAsk five lapsed testers why they stopped.

Send a short, friendly question to Beta testers who went quiet: what were you hoping for, and what stopped you? Answers beat charts at this size. Group the replies into three or four reasons and fix the most common one.

4Fix, retest, decideChange one thing, then read the next cohort.

Make one product or message change, record the date, and compare the cohort that arrives after it with the one before. If two honest tries change nothing, the issue is probably the product itself, and the right move is to improve what the apps give, not to promote harder.

Revisit this lesson when the counter is fixed and you have a few weeks of cohorts. That is also the point to decide on a mail service, a weekly digest, and whether the HV Reset mobile app plan makes real reminders possible.
