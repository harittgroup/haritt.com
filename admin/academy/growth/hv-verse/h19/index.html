What a growth audit and a weekly rhythm do for you

A check of where you stand, then a small habit that keeps you honest.

AuditA snapshot

Rate 12 areas, rank the fixes. Repeat every quarter.

vs
Weekly rhythmA habit

Five numbers, one learning, one decision each week.

1
Before a gym owner buys new machines, they look at which classes are full, which members quit and why. A growth audit is that check for your business. You go through the areas that decide whether growth works and give each one a rating: Strong (it works, is measured and can be repeated), Building (it exists but is partial or unmeasured) or Gap (missing or broken). You finish with a scorecard and a ranked list of fixes. The point is to see where your next hour pays off most, instead of chasing a new channel while the basics leak.
2
A weekly rhythm is the other half. Growth rarely fails because of bad ideas. It fails because nobody owns the number, nobody meets regularly, and the data sits in five places. A cricket side works because everyone knows their role and someone owns the result. A growth team is the same, even when the team is two people. Yours is: you and your co-founder, with no marketing hire and no fixed roles written down, and no weekly review on record.
3
For HV Verse, the audit tells you the truth about a young launch, and the rhythm turns that truth into action every week. Neither costs money.
Do you need this now? Yes, both. The audit takes a few hours and gives you a starting point to compare with later. The weekly rhythm takes a short slot each week. Without them, the other lessons are separate ideas with nobody checking whether they worked.

Do the audit in a Google Sheet with one row per area. It takes an afternoon the first time. Use the order below.

1Collect evidence for each rowA rating without proof is a mood.

For each of the twelve areas, save one thing that proves the rating: a screenshot of the dashboard, a link, a report, an export of the beta applications. If you cannot find any, that is your answer: Gap, "not measured yet".

2Rate honestlyThree ratings only.

Strong means it works, is measured and can be repeated. Building means it exists but is partial or unmeasured. Gap means missing or broken. Resist the urge to round up. A truthful Gap is more useful than a flattering Building.

3Write the gap and tie a fix to a lessonSo the audit leads to action.

In the next column write the gap in one sentence, then link a fix to the lesson that explains it, such as measurement, tagged links, activation or ethics. Then give each fix a score for impact and ease from 1 to 5, multiply or add them, and rank. Your own plan says to pick the three with the highest score.

4Close the foundation firstFix the foundation before growth work.

Your files say the foundation is incomplete: activation, retention reading and the beta assets need fixing before growth work pays off. Skipping to growth is not advised, because nothing is measurable yet. Close that list, then audit again once the counter is fixed.

A review that happens once is a task. One that happens every week is a rhythm. Pick a fixed day and a fixed length, and keep it short. Your own plan is one weekly review of five numbers, one learning log entry and one batch of posts. Here is a routine that fits two people. Menu names in the dashboard can change, so use the picture as a guide.

1Open the numbersSame slot, same place.

Start with the dashboard: the Ecosystem tab, then the funnel for each app. Use the same date range each time, such as the last 7 days against the 7 days before.

2Write the five numbersOnly five.

Suggested set: activated users per app, new users still active after 7 days (once fixed), shared scorecard visits, visits by tagged source, and minutes spent on outreach. Copy them into the sheet. If one is not available, write "not measured".

3Find the weakest funnel stepWeakest step first.

For each app, name the one step that loses the most people. Do not try to fix all of them. Pick one.

4Review last week's test and log one learningLearning is what you keep.

Open the log. What did you change last week? What happened? Write one sentence, even if the answer is "nothing moved".

5Choose next week's test and its ownerOne owner, one date.

Pick one test from your ranked list. Write the guess, who runs it and when you will look. Then plan the post batch and the daily half hour of outreach.

6Write who owns whatRoles prevent drift.

Your plan suggests one of you owns product and the other owns outreach. Write that down, plus who prepares the review and who logs it. Where a task is shared, use a simple grid with four labels: who does it (Responsible), who answers for it (Accountable), who is asked (Consulted) and who is told (Informed). With two people it is a few lines.

1
A monthly slot adds a look at channels and effort (see the cost maths lesson), and a quarterly slot repeats the audit and checks the strategy. Write a one-page how-to for any task you repeat, so that either of you can do it.
2
On tools, keep the stack small and give each number one home. You already have your own admin dashboard, Firebase, Google Apps Script with Gmail, Cloudflare, GitHub Pages and GA4. What you do not have is a CRM (a tool that tracks contacts), an email service, a post scheduler or a Search Console export. Free options exist for each of these, such as Google Sheets or Notion for the log, and Google Search Console for search data, but add one only when a real need appears and with your agreement. A new tool that nobody opens is worse than none.
HHow to read the review numbersWhat each number means and what to do when it looks bad.

Activated per app. This is the count from the Activated column. Compare it with last week, same range. Up with the same hours means something worked. Flat or down while your hours went up means change the approach. Never add the three rows into one number of people.

Share of sessions from shared scorecards. This is a quiet sign of word of mouth. If it is zero even though students saved scorecards, the share links may carry no label, so the loop is invisible. The action is to tag the scorecard share link.

Retention cells. Read a cell only when it holds a percent. Write the cohort size beside it. A D1 that falls to near zero by D7 means interest ended fast, so ask testers why. While Returning still shows 0, skip this row and note that it is not trusted.

Weakest funnel step. Divide each funnel row by the row above and name the lowest. The action is one change aimed at that step, logged with the date.

When the rhythm or the audit stalls

Why reviews fade, and how to see what is blocking growth.

What is going on?

Tap what you see. The likely culprit shows here.
Most likelyToo long or no owner

Cut to five numbers, fix the slot, name who prepares it.

Most likelyNo decision step

End every review with one test and one owner.

Most likelyToo many changes or too few

Run one test, hold the rest, and wait a fair period.

Most likelyNo single source

Keep one sheet and one dashboard as the source for each number.

Most likelyFixes not scheduled

Take the top three and put them on the calendar.

1
Rhythms fade for dull reasons. The meeting becomes long, the numbers are hard to find, or nobody leaves with a decision. Fix those, not the people. Cut the review to the shortest version that still ends with one test, one owner and one date. Keep one source of truth for each number, so you never spend the slot arguing about which figure is right.
2
If a test shows no change, that is still information. Log it, and do not call it a failure. If a test shows a change, ask whether something else changed too, such as a post going viral in a group chat. Repeat before you scale, and stop the ones that do not move the number after a fair try.
3
If the audit itself feels pointless, check whether the evidence column is empty. An audit built on opinion repeats what you already believed. Go back to the screenshots and exports. And if two ratings seem to contradict each other, for example measurement Building while experimentation is Gap, remember that they depend on each other: you cannot run a fair test until the counts can be trusted, so the measurement fix comes first.
When to come back: repeat the audit once the counter is fixed and you have some real numbers, then every quarter. After three months of weekly numbers, use them to decide what to do about ads. Until then, the habit itself is the win.
