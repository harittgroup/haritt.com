Who it is for, and what you offer

Pick one first group and one promise. Everything else in growth rests on these two choices.

TodayFour apps, several groups

Students, freshers, institutes, Grade 10 pupils.

Needed firstOne person, one promise

A sentence you can say to one student.

1
Think of the tuition class that says "we teach maths" against the one that says "Class 10 students who dread algebra get it right in eight weeks". The second one is easier to find, easier to believe and easier to tell a friend about. Two ideas sit behind that. Your ideal customer is the one group you aim at first, the people your product fits best. Your offer (also called the value proposition) is the plain promise to that group: what they get, how quickly, and what risk you remove. For HV Verse the offer is free, so the risk is already low, but the promise still has to be clear.
2
This matters more for you than for most, because every later choice depends on it: which words you use in a bio, which poster you print, which Instagram post you make first, which college group you join. If the target is "all students", each of those choices becomes a guess. Today the files show a social plan that names students aged 16 to 22 and freshers aged 21 to 26, marked as a draft. The review of your own site found that all four apps quietly point at job seekers, but no first group has been chosen. The pitch deck for coaching institutes then aims at a third group, pupils up to Grade 10.
Do you need this now? Yes, and it is the cheapest step in the whole academy because it costs nothing but a decision. HV Verse launched on 1 October, the Student Beta is the first real push, and the beta page has no source tracking yet, so every post you make before choosing a group is harder to judge later. Do this before you design posters, post a series on Instagram or approach any college.

The rest of this lesson walks through four things in order: what must be true before you choose, how to choose and write the promise, how to test whether students actually need the product, and what to do when the answers disappoint. It also covers the word lead, because in a free product the word means something different from a shop.

If you are honest, the answer to most of these is "not yet". That is fine. It only means the first choice is a working guess that you will confirm in the next two parts, not a final decision.

Choosing the group and writing the promise

Compare the three real options, then write a promise a student can repeat.

Option 1Fresher job seekers

Aged 21 to 26. One story across all four apps.

Option 2College students

Aged 16 to 22. Reached through groups and the beta.

Option 3Institutes first

You have three realistic paths, and each one comes from your own files. None is wrong, but they cost very different amounts of effort and risk. Read them as a comparison, and keep in mind that you are choosing a first group, not a forever group. The others go on a waiting list.

OptionWhy it could workWhy it could failWhat it needs first
Fresher job seekersReset, Hunt, Assess and AI all help someone searching for work. One story covers every app.Hard to find in groups unless you are in their job communities.A promise about the job search, and Hunt working well for the first ten users.

My reading: fresher job seekers is the cleanest first choice, because all four apps genuinely help that person and the story is the same everywhere. College students are the easiest to reach right now, so you can run both at once as long as the promise differs. But this is my reading, not a fact on file, so test it against what the Signups page shows.

TThe jobs-to-be-done sentenceDescribe the situation, not the age.

Age is a weak description. A better one is the moment when someone needs your product. The usual form is: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." For example, "When I have ten job applications going and I forget which stage each is at, I want one place to see them, so I can follow up on time." That is the job HV Hunt does. Write one sentence like this per app, using words students actually said to you.

TThe promise and the proofA promise a student can repeat after one hearing.

Write a single line: "For [group] who [job], HV Verse gives [result] in [time], unlike [what they use now], because [reason to believe]." The reason to believe can be a real screen from the app. Then test it: show the line to a student for five seconds and ask what HV Verse is. If they cannot say it back, rewrite it. Since the apps are free, you do not need risk reversal like a refund. Your risk reversal is that they can try it without paying and with data they can download.

Pick five people from the Signups page or your own contacts. Ask for ten minutes by WhatsApp or call. Ask about the last time they felt lost about their studies, a job search or a week plan. HHow to read the numbers on these screensWhat each number on these screens means and what to do.

Number of answers. This is the total of the three choices on the poll screen. It tells you how much weight to give the rest. With 20 answers one student moves the share by 5 points, so treat the result as a hint. Under about 10, write the answers down and read them as a conversation rather than as a percentage. If few testers answer, the poll is probably hard to find or shown to the wrong people. Move it, or message the testers directly.

Share who are very disappointed. Divide the Very disappointed count by all answers. In the made-up example 7 of 20 is 35 percent. A common rule of thumb says 40 percent or more points to real need, but it is a rule of thumb, not a law. Read it together with the open answers. A low share with strong words from a small group often means you have found your first group and the rest are not yet for you.

Split by group. In the Sheets tally, filter column B and repeat the COUNTIF. A pattern worth acting on looks like this: final year students answer very disappointed far more often than first year students. The action is to aim your promise and posters at the group that scores, and put the others on a waiting list. If every group scores the same and low, the problem is the product or promise, not the audience.

Status counts on Signups. Count rows per status each week. Many rows stuck in new for more than a week means people applied and nobody replied, so they cool off. The action is a weekly slot for replies. Many contacted but few joined points to the follow up message or the next step being unclear. The profession column tells you who applies without any push, which is free evidence for your first group.

What the Signups page cannot tell you. It stores no source, so a spike in applications cannot be tied to a poster. Tag every link with the Links tab, then read visits to the beta page by campaign in your Analytics tab, and ask one extra question on the form later.

Part 5 of 7

Read the results and tell if it worked

Fit signals, false positives and the three kinds of lead.

7 of 20Counts toward fit
9 of 20Likes it, not essential
4 of 20Could live without

Made-up example with 20 answers, only to show how to read the result.

Product-market fit sounds large, but for you it simply means: do enough students truly need what you made, and does your message make one group respond? A widely used rule of thumb is the Sean Ellis test, which is the question you just added. If at least 40 out of 100 users say "very disappointed", you have probably reached fit. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, and it needs a reasonable number of answers. With twenty answers, treat it as a hint.

In the made-up example above, 7 of 20 is 35 percent. That is close, so you would not celebrate or panic. You would read the open answers, check which group gave the "very disappointed" replies, and ask whether that group is the one you were going to aim at. Often the answer is surprising: the people who care most are not the group you chose.

TThree signals to read togetherOne number alone can mislead.

Survey: the fit question above. Behaviour: do people come back without a reminder? Your dashboard has D1, D7, D14 and D30 retention by first-visit day, plus weekly and monthly active devices, but the 13 October screenshot showed zeros for returning visitors, which looked like a counting bug. Fix that before trusting retention. Pull: do people arrive through friends or search without you pushing? Look at the Channels view for visits marked as shared by users.

WWhat counts as a lead for youA lead is a person with a reason to come back.

With no sale, the word changes. Write three kinds of lead on one page. The user lead is someone who passed activation: first task done in HV Reset, first job added in HV Hunt, test completed in HV Assess. The applicant lead is a Student Beta application, tracked by status. The institute lead is an organisation that might use the apps with students. Today there is no way to capture institute leads, so use a simple sheet or a short form until volume justifies more.

FFalse positives to avoidPraise that looks like fit but is not.

Friends and family saying "nice" is not fit. A burst of sign-ups from one WhatsApp forward is not fit. High visits with no repeat use is not fit. A single enthusiastic user who never returns is not fit. Count only people who did the core action.

When the answers disappoint

Check the message, the group, the product and the tracking, in that order.

What do your numbers show?

Tap what you see. The likely culprit shows here.
Most likelyWeak fit or wrong group

Split the answers by profession. If one group scores high, aim there. If none does, talk to the low scorers.

Most likelyWrong audience for the question

You asked people who never finished a first task, or the poll is hard to find. Narrow the audience and move it.

Most likelyPromise does not match the first minute

Open the app as a new user. Does the first screen deliver what the post promised?

Most likelyNice to have, not a need

Ask what they would use if the app disappeared. If it is nothing, the need may be weak.

Most likelyTracking gap

Tag the links and add a source to the application before judging any channel.

Fit is rarely a clean yes or no. When the signals are weak, you do not give up. You find out whether the problem is the group, the message, the product or the measurement, because each has a different fix.

1Group problemRight product, wrong people.

Split the survey and applications by profession. If fresher job seekers score well on HV Hunt but school students do not, you have found your first group. Fix: aim the promise at the group that scores, and move the others to a waiting list.

2Message problemPeople do not understand what you offer.

Run the five-second test on three students. If they describe the product differently from how you do, the words are wrong. Fix: use their words. Retest by showing the new line to three new students.

3Product problemPeople understand it but do not get value.

Watch one new user open the app on a call, without helping. Where do they stop? Fix the first minute before anything else. Retest with the next five testers.

4Measurement problemThe data cannot answer the question.

If returning visitors show zero while visits clearly repeat, the counter may be wrong. Fix it, then compare your own counter against GA4. Until they roughly agree, do not decide on retention.

Change one thing at a time and give each change a week or a set number of new testers. Then ask the fit question again to the new testers only. If the share rises, keep the change. If it does not, undo it and try the next cause.

Keep this short and concrete. First, read the Signups page and note which group is already applying. Second, add the fit question and run the five short chats. Third, write a one-line promise for HV Verse and one for each app, and put the same words in your bios, posters and welcome mails. 2
When you revisit, bring three things to the table: the fit answers split by group, the weekly status counts from the Signups page, and your log of changes. If the same group keeps coming out on top, write that group into your growth plan as the one you serve first. If two groups tie, run one more round with a different promise for each and compare. Do not widen the target just because a new group looks exciting, since each new group needs its own words, its own places to reach them and its own proof.
What to focus on instead of more audiences: do not write personas for all three groups. One group, one promise and five real conversations will teach you more than a long document. The institute route can wait until you know the consent and sign-in answers.
