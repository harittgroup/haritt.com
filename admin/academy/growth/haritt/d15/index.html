1
Money maths means knowing what it costs you to win a client, what a client is worth, and how much you can afford to spend finding the next one. It sounds like something for big companies, but for a studio like Haritt Developers it matters more, not less. You sell a small number of large projects. One extra win, or one lost month, is a big share of your year. Guessing here is expensive.
2
Here is where you stand, from your files. Ad spend is zero, by decision, and the social plan says time is the budget. The only costs on record are the domain (about ₹370 for the first year) and your tools. The time of the two founders is the real cost, and it is not counted anywhere. No won project, no quote and no margin is recorded in any file, so a cost per client cannot be worked out today. Your form does ask for a budget band: Under ₹50,000, ₹50,000 to 1.5 lakh, ₹1.5 to 5 lakh, or Above ₹5 lakh. How enquirers answered is not on file.
3
Three words appear in this lesson. CAC (customer acquisition cost) is what it costs you to win one client. LTV (lifetime value) is what a client is worth to you over the whole relationship. Margin is what is left of a project's price after the cost of doing the work. Each one is explained with your own business below.
Do you need this now? You need the habit now, and the numbers later. Start writing down cost, time and project value this month. It takes a few minutes. You cannot calculate a useful cost per client until you have won at least a couple of projects, so do not worry about the answer yet. Do not plan any paid spending until the records exist. Your own plan says no paid ads for the first 90 days.

CProject value and marginQuote, price agreed and what it cost to deliver.

No quote or project value is on file. For each won project, record the agreed price and what the work cost you (tools, any paid help, and your hours at your fair value). The difference is the margin. If you do not know the cost of delivery, make your best estimate and say it is an estimate.

Cost per client (CAC)

Add up everything spent in a period, then divide by the projects won.

AddMoney plus time

Tools, domain, anything paid, and founder hours.

DivideBy projects won

Same period, same set of costs.

CompareWith the margin

Is the cost small next to what you earn?

The formula is plain. Take everything you spent on finding clients in a period, add the value of the time you spent, and divide by the number of projects you won in that period. Choose the period before you look at the result, such as a quarter, because wins come slowly and a single month can show zero.

AA worked example (made-up numbers)Example only. None of these figures is on file.

Say that in a quarter you spent ₹1,200 on tools and the domain, and the two of you spent 90 hours on marketing, valued at ₹300 an hour for this example. That time is ₹27,000. The total is ₹28,200. If you won two projects, your cost per client is ₹14,100. If you won none, there is no cost per client yet, and the honest sentence is "₹28,200 spent, no win yet". These are invented numbers to show the steps.

WWhy count your timeFree is not free.

If you ignore your time, every channel looks cheap. A post that takes six hours and brings one enquiry is not free. Valuing the hours is what lets you compare a post with, for example, a call with a referrer. Keep the hourly value the same each time. It is a tool for comparing, not a bill.

CCost per channelUse the source column.

When you have enough wins, split the total by source. If the cost per client from referrals is far lower than from LinkedIn posts, put more hours into referrals. With only a few wins this split is noisy, so treat it as a hint until you have more than a handful.

What a client is worth, and the sheet to track it

Margin on the first project, plus support or repeat work, minus the cost of winning.

For a project studio, a client is worth the margin on the first project, plus whatever comes later: support work, a second project, or an introduction to someone else. Today only the first is on your files, as Consulting and Support is one of your seven services but no client list or repeat record exists. So begin with the simple version: the value of a client is the margin on the project.

A note on honesty with yourself. The easy mistake is to count only what is easy to count. Tool bills are easy. Hours are not, so they get left out, and the cost looks tiny. Another mistake is to count a project as won on the day the client says yes, while the work and the payment come months later. Pick one rule, such as counting a win when the agreement is signed, and use it every time. Write the rule at the top of the Money tab so that next quarter you do not change it by accident.

Open your sheet, add a tab called Money, and set it out as the screen above shows. In the total cost cell, add the SUM of the amount column and then the hours times your fixed hourly value. In the projects won cell, count the Won rows on your Leads tab. For cost per client, divide the first by the second, and wrap it so that zero wins shows a dash instead of an error. Menu names and formula help can change, so use the sheet's own help if a function is missing.

AA worked example (made-up numbers)Example only: price ₹60,000, delivery cost ₹30,000.

Say a project is agreed at ₹60,000 and it costs you ₹30,000 to deliver, counting your hours. The margin is ₹30,000. If the cost to win it was ₹14,100 as above, you keep ₹15,900 from the first project. If the client later buys a support plan or sends a friend, that adds to the value. Compare cost per client with margin. If winning costs more than the project earns, the channel is too expensive no matter how many enquiries it brings.

Working backwards, and the budget

Start from the projects you want and see how many visits you would need.

1,0004 in 100 enquire

Made-up example. Every rate is invented. Today's site has about 300 visits a month.

Working backwards is the most honest way to set a target. Decide how many projects you want a month, then divide by each step's rate to see how much has to enter the top. In the made-up example above, two wins a month would need about 1,000 visits at those invented rates. Your site gets about 300. The gap is not a reason to panic. It shows which rate is worth improving first, and that a better first reply or a stronger case study may help more than more visitors.

BBudget: time firstTreat your hours as the budget, then add a small capped test only if you choose.

Your budget today is time. Split it like a portfolio: most hours to free capture (your profile, listings and replies), some to proof (one case study), and a small slice to one experiment. Any money spent needs your approval and a stop-loss, which is a limit at which you stop. Write the rule before you spend: for example, "If this test has produced no qualified lead by the amount I set, I stop." The paid question for Developers is still open on your plan.

Today no price appears on the site. Quotes are custom, there are no packages, and the FAQ only mentions a smaller starting scope. Buyers who do not see any price may assume you are out of reach, or may waste your time with a budget you cannot serve. The form's budget bands help you see this after the fact. Review what enquirers choose before you decide anything.

1Check the recordsAre the numbers right?

Before you blame anything, check that the cost log and the Won rows are complete. A missing project or a forgotten tool skews the answer.

2Check the leak, not the totalWhich step is losing people?

If cost per client is high because few enquiries are qualified, the fix is the audience or the offer. If it is high because proposals are not accepted, it is price, scope or proof. The pipeline stages show which.

3Test a price signalOne change at a time.

A package with a fixed scope and a "from" price for your most requested service is a clean test. Show it on the services page after your OK, then watch whether the budget bands and qualified rate move over the next 10 to 15 enquiries. If they do not, remove it.

Scale, repeat or stop. If the cost per client is well below the margin, put more hours into that channel, slowly. If it is above the margin and a fix exists, change one thing and measure again. If it stays above after honest tries, stop that channel and use the hours elsewhere.

1
This month, create the Money tab and fill in what you already know: domain and tools. Pick your fair hourly value and keep it. Write down your weekly marketing hours. For every new quote, record the price, and for every win, the margin. Review once a quarter, not weekly, because a project studio has too few wins for short-term reading.
2
Come back to this lesson when you have two or three wins. At that point your cost per client, your margin and the channel split start to mean something. Then you can answer the questions that paid advertising would force on you: how much could I spend to win one client and still come out ahead? Until then, the free work you are doing is the right use of your time, and the records are what turn it from effort into evidence.
3
One more habit helps. At the end of each quarter, write three lines in your notes: what you spent in money and hours, what you won and at what margin, and the one number you want to move next quarter. Keep each quarter's three lines in the same place. After a year you will have four short entries that tell you more about your studio than any general advice could, and they will make the decision about paid ads a calm one instead of a guess.
