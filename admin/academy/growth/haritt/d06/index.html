Why introductions suit a small studio

A trusted person saying your name does what no ad can: it lends you their credibility.

vs
An introduced visitorArrives with a reason to trust

1
Imagine an accountant who looks after the books of fifty small businesses. One of them mentions that the website is old and loses customers. The accountant says, "I know a team that builds these." That one sentence is a referral. Compare it with a stranger who finds you through a search: the stranger has to be convinced, while the introduced owner already half believes you. For a studio selling custom projects with no packages or listed prices, that borrowed trust is the most valuable kind of lead you can get.
2
Your files name referrers as one of three audiences in the social plan: accountants, designers and printers. These people meet small business owners before you do and have no reason to build websites themselves. Three terms are worth knowing. A referral is a client or partner sending someone your way. A partner is a business that regularly sees your kind of buyer. Community is a group of people with a shared interest, such as a local business owners' WhatsApp group. An influencer or affiliate programme (people paid to promote you) is another idea in this lesson's list, and your files say it suits a studio sale poorly. The closest useful version is the referral partner.
3
This lesson shows how to find partners, ask in a way they can act on, give them a link you can track, and judge whether the effort pays.
Do you need this now? Yes, in a small and careful way. Referrals cost no money, they suit a service sold by trust, and you can start with people you already know. What is missing is not the idea but the machinery: no referral ask exists, no tracked link goes to partners, and the form has no field for how someone heard of you. Build those three things first, then ask.

What must be in place first

A partner who introduces you is putting their own name at risk. If the owner they sent gets silence, a muddled pitch or a slow reply, the partner will not do it again. So the sensible order is to prepare, then ask. Here is what to check, with where you stand from your files.

ASomething concrete to point toA partner needs a story to repeat.

From your files, the only work shown is in-house, including HV Verse, and there are no client case studies yet. A partner can still say "they built and launched an app suite in October 2026", which is real. Word of mouth gets much easier after one delivered client project, so the first delivered project is also the best moment to ask for an introduction.

How to check: ask yourself whether a partner could explain what you do and for whom in one breath, and whether a link exists that they could forward.

BOne sentence a partner can repeatWithout it, introductions are vague.

Your files say no ideal client is chosen. For referrals that matters directly, because a partner can only pass on someone who fits the sentence. Something like "they build websites and simple apps for small local businesses" is easy to repeat. "Digital experiences that help businesses grow" is not.

How to check: read the sentence aloud to a friend outside tech, and ask them who they would send. If they name the wrong people, rewrite it. You choose the wording, since only you know which clients you want.

CA tagged link per partnerSo you know who sent whom.

A UTM tag is a small label added to a link, such as source and campaign names, that analytics records. If each partner gets their own tagged link, visits show up in your dashboard by partner. Your admin analytics has a Links tab that makes such links, including a channel for friend referral.

How to check: create a test link, click it, and see the visit under the right source.

DA way to record the source on the formVisits alone are not enquiries.

Your enquiry form saves a fixed src and no UTM, so a lead cannot be tied to a channel. The simplest fix is a plain question: "How did you hear about us?" with the option to name who referred them. Changing the form is a site change, so it needs your approval and the person who maintains the site. Until then, ask in your first reply to every enquiry.

How to check: open the form as a visitor and look for any field or hidden tag that records where you came from.

EA reward rule, decided in advanceKeep it free until you decide otherwise.

Your files suggest returning introductions as the starting reward, which costs nothing. A paid thank-you fee needs your approval and care, since a fee can turn a friendly gesture into a commission with legal and tax questions. Decide before the first conversation so you do not improvise.

How to check: write the rule in one line and keep it with your notes.

Planning who to ask

A short list of ten, picked for who they meet, not how big they are.

AccountantsKnow who is struggling

They see the business finances.

DesignersNeed a builder

Logo and print work, then a website.

PrintersMeet new shops

Signage and cards for new businesses.

The best partners meet your buyers at the moment a website becomes a need, and they do not compete with you. Your files name three types, and each suits a slightly different conversation. Start with people you already know, because a warm message gets answered and a cold one often does not. The goal of the first round is learning, not volume.

AAccountants and finance advisersThey hear the problem before anyone.

They know which clients are starting out, expanding, or losing money, and they are trusted. A good introduction from them is usually to an owner who has just asked "how do I get more customers?" Offer them something useful too: a short plain note they can forward when a client's site is outdated.

DDesigners and branding peopleThey often do the design but not the build.

A designer who creates a logo or brand for a small business often is asked about the site. If they do not build sites, they need someone dependable. This relationship can run both ways: you may also send them clients who need branding. Reciprocal introductions are the cheapest form of partnership.

PPrinters and signage makersThey meet businesses at the start.

Someone ordering a signboard or visiting cards is often opening a shop. Printers see this early and rarely have a digital answer. A small card with your tagged link works well here, since printers have counters where cards sit.

WWhere community and chat fitLocal owners respond faster on WhatsApp.

Your files say no community or chat channel exists for Developers, and a click-to-chat button is not on the site. For local owners a quick WhatsApp message often beats a form. Joining existing local business groups as a helpful member is free; posting promotions in them often backfires. Offer answers first.

Write the list of ten with one line each: who they are, which type, what they could pass on, and why they would care. This is the plan, and it fits half a page.

Asking, step by step

From the list to a first conversation, using your own link tool.

2ChannelChoose the friend referral option or the closest match.
3CampaignName the partner or round, such as 202610_partners.
4Content tagAdd the partner's short name so you see who sent what.
5Generated linkGive this to the partner, never the plain address.

Now do the work. Take it one partner at a time, because each message should show you thought about them. The tool you need is the link maker in your admin analytics, which already exists. As always, menu names can change, so look for the link maker if a tab is renamed.

1Make one tagged link per partnerAbout two minutes each.

In the Links tab pick the page, pick the channel, put the partner's short name in the content tag, and copy the result. Keep a small sheet with partner, date, link and what you asked. That sheet is your referral record, and you can export your admin inbox as a CSV (a plain spreadsheet file) to match later.

2Send a short, specific messageA reason, an ask, and an easy next step.

Say who you are, why you are writing to them in particular, what you do in one sentence, and one small ask, such as "if a client ever asks about a website, could you pass on this link?" Offer something in return, such as sending them clients who need their kind of work. Nothing automatic: send these yourself, one by one.

3Make forwarding effortlessGive them a ready-made two-line note.

Many partners are willing but busy. Give them text they can paste into a message to their client, with the tagged link inside. The easier it is, the more often it happens.

4Reply fast and report backTell the partner what happened.

When an introduced owner enquires, reply within one working day. After the first call, thank the partner, and say what came of it, without sharing private details. Partners who see results keep sending. Add a "referred by" note to the inbox entry so the record exists even though the form lacks a field.

5Ask happy clients tooThe best moment is right after a win.

After a delivered project, once the client is pleased, ask for one introduction. Draft two lines in advance and list five clients or partners to ask. Count enquiries that say "referred by" in the first message.

With ten partners you will not get statistics. You will get stories, and that is fine. The record you keep should let you see a chain from each partner to what followed: asked, replied, passed on a link, visits arrived, enquiry sent, call held, project won. The first three steps live in your sheet, visits in the Campaigns table of the developers tab, and enquiries in the admin inbox. Calls and wins are not tracked yet, since the inbox has only New, Contacted and Closed. Add notes or a column in your sheet for those.

Compare against the other channels in your dashboard once you have a month of data: a referral lead that costs an hour of messaging is cheap beside most alternatives.

When partners do not send anyone

Find the broken link in the chain: list, ask, link, reply, fit or proof.

When a round of referrals gives little, avoid the easy guess that partners just are not interested. The chain has six links and each leaves a different mark in your sheet. Check them from the earliest to the latest, and stop where the chain first breaks.

1Did the right people see the ask?Look at replies versus messages sent.

If few answer, check whether you picked people who actually meet small business owners, and whether the first line shows you know their world. A generic message gets generic silence. Try a different ten, or a shorter message.

2Was passing it on easy?Compare replies with visits.

Warm replies but no visits mean the partner meant well and did nothing. Give them the ready note and link, and tell them the one situation in which to use it. Make sure your tagged link opens on a phone without errors.

3Did the visitor find a reason to trust you?Compare visits with enquiries.

If introduced owners visit and leave, the page may be asking for too much too soon. Add proof near the form (see the lesson on proof), and consider a click-to-chat button, since a quick WhatsApp message is easier than a form with budget and timeline fields.

4Did you answer well?Compare enquiries with calls held.

Check how many hours passed between each enquiry and your reply. Referred owners expect quick, personal attention. A delay is the most common way to waste a free lead.

To retest, change one thing at a time, for example only the message text for the next five partners. Write down what you believed, what you changed, and the result, and compare after the next round. Stop asking people who have given a clear no, and do not repeat a request more than twice.

1
Referrals compound slowly. A partner who sends one good client and hears the result will send another, and a happy client who is asked at the right time often knows two more people like them. Your job is to make each step easy and each outcome visible.
2
Keep a small partner list with last contact, what you sent them, and what they sent you. Review it every few months. Drop the quiet ones without hard feelings and spend time on the two or three that work. Return introductions are free and remain the best reward.
3
Revisit the idea of a paid referral fee only when referrals bring real revenue and you have the figures to judge it, and only after you approve it. Revisit a community of your own only when you have regular things to say. Until then, the focus is the same as in the proof lesson: a finished project, an honest story and a quick reply.
