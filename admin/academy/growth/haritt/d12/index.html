What testing means, and why a formal A/B test is not for you yet

A test is a fair comparison. At about 10 visits a day, you test bold changes and talk to people instead.

Formal A/B testNeeds thousands of visits

Splits visitors at random and compares one number.

vs
Your kind of testBold change, careful read

Before and after, plus real conversations.

1
Say you change your Start a project headline and the next week brings three enquiries instead of one. Did the headline work? You cannot tell. A festival, a good post or plain luck could have done it. A test is a way of making the comparison fair. In an A/B test, visitors are split at random: half see the current page (the control) and half see the changed one (the variant), at the same time. Because the split is random, a gap between them can be blamed on your change, and statistics tells you whether the gap is bigger than chance.
2
The catch is volume. For the last 30 days to 13 Oct you had 301 sessions, about 10 a day, and your enquiry rate is not on file. Here is a worked example with made-up numbers. Say your enquiry rate is 5 percent and you want to detect a rise to 7 percent. A common rule of thumb for the visitors needed per version is 16 times the variance divided by the square of the smallest change you care about. The variance of a rate is p times (1 minus p), so 0.05 times 0.95 is 0.0475. The smallest change is 0.02, squared 0.0004. That gives 16 times 0.0475 divided by 0.0004, which is 1,900 visitors per version, so 3,800 in all. At 10 a day that is about 380 days, more than a year.
Do you need formal A/B testing now? No, later. At this traffic a classic test cannot finish in a useful time, and calling noise a win would mislead you. But the habit behind testing is needed now: write down a guess, change one thing, compare fairly, and keep notes. The rest of this lesson shows how to do that with small numbers, so every change you make teaches you something.

A test is only as good as what it is measured with. Nothing here is expensive, but most items are missing today, which is why testing is not yet the right use of your time.

The first requirement is trustworthy counting. The previous lessons set up GA4 and your own counter so that enquiry_sent matches the inbox. If that gap exists, any test result is meaningless. The second is a baseline: your enquiry rate over at least four whole weeks before the change, which tells you what normal looks like. The third is a nearer metric. Since sent enquiries are rare, many teams measure something that happens more often, such as cta_contact or form_start, both tracked on your site. They move sooner, so you learn sooner.

1A learning logA sheet with one row per idea.

Columns: ID, dates, hypothesis and the evidence behind it, what you changed, the primary metric, a guardrail, the result, the decision and what you now believe. Keep failed tests too. A log is how you avoid repeating an idea that already failed six months ago.

2A primary metric and a guardrailOne number decides, another must not fall.

The primary metric might be form starts per contact page visit. A guardrail is a number that must not get worse, such as the share of starters who finish, or enquiry quality. A change that raises starts but lowers finishes is not a win.

3A decision rule written before you startShip, drop or repeat.

For example: "If starts rise by at least a quarter over four weeks and finishes hold, keep it. If they fall, undo it. If it is unclear, run it again for four more weeks." Writing this first stops you from reading the result the way you hoped.

1
A hypothesis is a written guess you can check. The format is: because [what I saw], if we [change], then [metric] will [move] by [amount], measured over [period]. Without it, "let us try something" turns into random edits. Here is one built from your files: "Because no lead tells us how it found us, if the form asks how they heard about us and saves the UTM source, we will know the channel for the next 20 enquiries." The guardrail: form completion must not drop.
2
You will always have more ideas than time. ICE scoring helps you pick. For each idea, give a score from 1 to 10 for Impact (how much it could move the number), Confidence (how sure you are, based on data rather than hope) and Ease (how little work it takes). How to read the ICE sheet. The score ranks ideas, it does not measure them. Scores within a point are a tie, so take the easier one. Part 4 of 7

Small traffic methods that actually work

Bold changes, a nearer metric, conversations, recordings and careful before and after reads.

Bigger changesOffer and structure

Not button colour. A big effect shows up with fewer visits.

Nearer metricform_start, cta_contact

They happen more often than a sent enquiry.

Talk and watchFive people, Clarity

A few conversations reveal most real problems.

When you cannot run a proper split, change what you test rather than pretend a weak test gave a firm answer. These methods are in the order I would use them.

1Talk to every enquirerThe cheapest research you have.

Each enquiry is precious. In your first reply, ask two things: what did you search for or where did you see us, and what nearly stopped you from writing. Log the answers in the inbox notes. Five such answers often teach more than a test at your traffic. Do the same with a few people who started the form and did not send it, if you can reach them.

2Watch real visitsMicrosoft Clarity recordings.

If Clarity is installed (see the enquiry page lesson), watch five to ten recordings of the contact page each month. Note where people hesitate, scroll past the form or leave. The notes become new hypotheses.

3Make one bold change at a timeLarge enough to see.

Replace the headline and proof together, or give your top service its own page, rather than changing a word. A big change either shows up in your numbers within a few weeks or it clearly does not.

4Read before and after carefullyWith a control if you can.

Compare four whole weeks before with four weeks after. Write down every other event in those weeks: a new listing, a post, a holiday. If you changed one page, compare it with a similar page you left alone. Say how unsure you are in the log. With so few enquiries, one extra lead can make a change look like a win.

5Accept more risk when undoing is cheapA headline can be changed back in minutes.

Where a change is easy to reverse, act on a smaller signal. Where it is costly, such as rebuilding a page, ask for stronger evidence first.

Later, when you run paid ads, small ad campaigns become another way to test messages cheaply, because you can see which promise gets clicks before building a page. Your plan says no paid ads for the first 90 days, so keep that for after the numbers are steady.

Here is the whole loop with the top idea from the list. The numbers in it are made up to show the process.

1Write it downBefore changing anything.

Hypothesis: because no lead says how it found us, if the form asks "How did you hear about us?" and saves the UTM values, then within about 8 weeks we can name the channel for most leads. Primary metric: the share of new enquiries with a known source. Guardrail: form completion must not fall. Decision rule: keep if the form completion stays within a small margin of the baseline.

2Record the baselineFour weeks, no changes.

From the dashboard and inbox: sessions, form_start, enquiry_sent and completion. Say you find 12 starts and 6 sends over four weeks (made up). Completion is half.

3Ship the change and note the dateAsk in the website thread.

Give the exact field text and the hidden fields. Put the date on the dashboard note. Test it yourself in DebugView and in the inbox record before you call it live.

4Wait and do not editPeeking breaks the test.

Check the tracking weekly, but do not alter the field or other parts of the page. Do not stop early because week one looks good or bad.

5Decide, log and pick the nextEven when nothing changed.

Compare after four weeks with the baseline. If completion held and most leads carry a source, keep it. If completion dropped, make the field optional or shorter and run again. Write what you learned in the log and take the next idea from the list.

HHow to read the baselineWhat the baseline numbers mean and what to do.

Started form and Sent enquiry. Started happens more often, so it moves sooner. Sent is the number that pays. If starts rise and sends do not, the form is losing people, so look at the fields and error messages. If both stay flat after a bold change, the leak is elsewhere in the funnel.

Form completion. Divide Sent by Started. In the example, 6 of 12 is 50 percent. With so few events one extra send moves it by 8 points, so compare four week blocks and write the counts beside the percent.

Enquiry rate. Sent divided by device days. Use it as a rough guide only. Never compare it with another site.

A fair read. Note every other event in those weeks, such as a post or a listing. If the numbers differ by less than a few events, write unclear in the log and run it again or try a bolder change.

Part 6 of 7

The monthly review: keeping the log alive

Once a month, read the log, rescore the list and decide what to stop, keep or try.

Read the logRead enquirer answersRescore ideasPick next change
1
A learning log only helps if you read it. Set one fixed day each month, put it next to your weekly dashboard habit, and spend half an hour on three things. This is where small tests add up into a clear picture of what your clients respond to.
2
First, read the log from the top. Which changes held, which were dropped, which were unclear? Look for patterns across rows, such as proof helping more than wording, or one channel always bringing better leads. Patterns are worth more than single results, because they tell you what to try next. Second, read all the answers enquirers gave to "How did you hear about us?" and "What nearly stopped you?", and write the three most common in one line each. Real words from clients are the best raw material for headlines and proof. Third, rescore the idea list. Some ideas become easier because tracking now works, and some lose confidence because a similar change failed.
3
Then choose exactly one change for the next month and write its hypothesis, metric, guardrail and decision rule before you touch the site. Also decide what to stop. If an idea has failed twice in different forms, drop it and record why. Revisit this lesson when traffic grows enough to run a formal test, when you start paid ads, or when you change your ideal client, since each of these changes what is worth testing.

When a change does not help: find out why, and when to stop

Separate a bad idea from a bad test, then decide to repeat, change or drop.

What do your numbers show?

Tap what you see. The likely culprit shows here.
Most likelyChange too small, or the leak is elsewhere

Look at the funnel again. Try a bolder version or a different step.

Most likelyNew friction at the form

Check the new field and the error messages. Watch recordings.

Most likelyA one-off spike

Check what else happened that week, such as a post or a listing.

Most likelyA mix problem

Segment by channel before deciding. A small group can swing the total.

Most likelyTracking or timing problem

Compare enquiry_sent with the inbox. Repeat on whole weeks.

Most likelyAudience or offer

Read the project text. Narrow the promise rather than the form.

1
A change that does not move the number is not a failure if you learn from it. The question is whether the idea was wrong or the test was too weak to tell. Use the patterns above to find out, and always begin by checking that the counting worked during the test period.
2
If the funnel shows the same drop as before, the change probably did not touch the real leak. Go back to the biggest drop and look again at recordings and conversations. If the result is mixed or the groups are tiny, the honest answer is "unclear". Run it again for another four weeks or choose a bolder version, and write "unclear" in the log rather than inventing a winner. If the number clearly got worse, undo the change, note the reason and move on to the next idea.
3
Know when to stop testing and do something else. If the funnel looks reasonable and traffic is still around 10 a day, the biggest lever is not the page. It is reach: search, Google Business Profile, listings, referrals. Revisit formal A/B testing when you have the baseline rate and enough traffic that a test fits in about eight weeks. A simple rule is to skip any test that needs longer than that. Until then, a steady habit of small, honest, well recorded changes is the right way to learn, and the log will show you its value within a few months.
